## Objective lop01

### Candidate A
#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **multiple** of a whole number is the product of that number and any whole‑number factor. For example, the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, … . A **common multiple** of two or more numbers is a number that appears in each of their multiple lists.

When looking for common multiples, it’s easy to forget one of the prime factors. A common slip is picking a number that contains some, but not all, of the required factors – for instance, choosing 9 as a common multiple of 2 and 3, which omits the factor 2. Remember: the candidate must be divisible by **each** original number.

_Sources: REF-1_

*worked_example*

**Worked Example 1:** Find the common multiples of 2 and 3.
1. List a few multiples of 2: **2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16** …
2. List a few multiples of 3: **3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18** …
3. Compare the two lists. The numbers that appear in **both** lists are **6** and **12** (and continue). Notice how each of these numbers is divisible by **both** 2 **and** 3. The choice “9” would be tempting because it is a multiple of 3, but it lacks the factor 2, so it is **not** a common multiple.

**Worked Example 2:** Find the common multiples of 4 and 6.
1. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32 …
2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36 …
3. Shared numbers: **12** and **24** …
4. The number 16 that was chosen earlier is only a multiple of 4; it does **not** contain the factor 3 needed for divisibility by 6, so it is not a common multiple.

_Sources: REF-2_

Identifying common multiples is the first step toward finding the **least common multiple (LCM)**.

_Sources: REF-1_

Notice that each of these numbers contains the factor 2 (from both numbers) **and** the factor 3 (from 6). A common mistake is to pick 16, which has the factor 2 but lacks the factor 3, so it is **not** a common multiple of 4 and 6.
4. Therefore, the common multiples of 4 and 6 are 12, 24, … .

## Objective lop02

### Candidate A
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each of the numbers. It is the first common multiple that appears when the multiples of the numbers are listed. This definition comes from the textbook chapter on Highest Common Factor and Lowest Common Multiple. (see REF‑1)

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – Finding the LCM of 12 and 18 using the prime‑factor method**

1. Write the prime factorisation of each number:
   - 12 = 2² × 3¹
   - 18 = 2¹ × 3²
2. For each distinct prime, take the highest power that appears:
   - For 2, the highest power is 2².
   - For 3, the highest power is 3².
3. Multiply these highest powers together:
   LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
Thus, the LCM of 12 and 18 is 36. (see REF‑2)

*practice*

**Practice Problems**

1. Find the LCM of 8 and 20.
   - Prime factors: 8 = 2³, 20 = 2² × 5¹
   - Highest powers: 2³ and 5¹
   - LCM = 2³ × 5¹ = 8 × 5 = **40**

2. Find the LCM of 15, 25 and 35.
   - Prime factors: 15 = 3¹ × 5¹, 25 = 5², 35 = 5¹ × 7¹
   - Highest powers: 3¹, 5², 7¹
   - LCM = 3¹ × 5² × 7¹ = 3 × 25 × 7 = **525**

Check your answers with the solutions provided in the textbook chapter on HCF and LCM. (see REF‑3)

*worked_example*

**Extension – LCM of Three Numbers (24, 30, 45) using the prime‑factor method**

1. Prime factorise each number:
   - 24 = 2³ × 3¹
   - 30 = 2¹ × 3¹ × 5¹
   - 45 = 3² × 5¹
2. Identify the highest power for each distinct prime:
   - 2: highest power is 2³ (from 24)
   - 3: highest power is 3² (from 45)
   - 5: highest power is 5¹ (from 30 or 45)
3. Multiply the highest powers together:
   LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ = 8 × 9 × 5 = **360**.
This demonstrates how the same method scales smoothly to more than two numbers. (see REF‑2)

**Challenge Problem**
