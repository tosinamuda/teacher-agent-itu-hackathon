#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **multiple** of a whole number is obtained by multiplying the number by any whole‑number factor. When two numbers share a multiple, that number is a **common multiple**. Recognising common multiples is the gateway to finding the least common multiple (LCM).

*worked_example*

**Worked Example:** Find a few common multiples of 5 and 8.
1. Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, …
2. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, …
3. The numbers appearing in both lists are 40 (and continue with 80, 120, …). Thus 40, 80, 120 are common multiples of 5 and 8.

### Candidate B
#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **common multiple** of two or more whole numbers is a number that is a multiple of each of those numbers. In other words, it can be divided by each of the numbers without leaving a remainder. For example, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because both 3 ÷ 12 and 4 ÷ 12 give whole‑number results. This definition underpins the task of identifying common multiples before finding the LCM.

#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

**Quick reminder:** The lowest common multiple (LCM) of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that each of the numbers divides evenly. This definition appears in the textbook chapter on Highest Common Factor and Lowest Common Multiple. (see REF‑1)

*explanation*

**Worked Example – LCM of 12 and 18 (prime‑factor method)**
1. Prime factorise: 12 = 2² × 3¹, 18 = 2¹ × 3².
2. Choose the highest power of each prime: 2² and 3².
3. Multiply: 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
This short recap follows the textbook’s step‑by‑step method. (see REF‑2)

### Candidate B
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each number. A *multiple* is a number divisible by another without remainder, and a *common multiple* is a multiple of two or more numbers. (see REF‑1, REF‑2)

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – Finding the LCM of 20 and 30 using the prime‑factor method**

1. Prime factorise each number:
   - 20 = 2² × 5¹
   - 30 = 2¹ × 3¹ × 5¹
2. For each distinct prime, take the highest power:
   - 2 → 2²
   - 3 → 3¹
   - 5 → 5¹
3. Multiply the highest powers: LCM = 2² × 3¹ × 5¹ = 4 × 3 × 5 = **60**.
Thus, the LCM of 20 and 30 is 60.

*practice*

**Practice Problems**

   - Highest powers: 2³, 3¹, 5¹
