## Objective lop01

### Candidate A
#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **multiple** of a whole number is a product you obtain by multiplying that number by any whole‑number factor. For example, the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, … because 4 × 1 = 4, 4 × 2 = 8, 4 × 3 = 12, etc.

A **common multiple** of two or more numbers is a number that appears in the list of multiples for each of those numbers. In other words, it is a number that both numbers divide into without leaving a remainder. For instance, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because both 3 × 4 = 12 and 4 × 3 = 12.

Understanding common multiples is the first step toward finding the least common multiple (LCM).

*worked_example*

**Worked Example 1 – Common multiples of 2 and 3**

1. List a few multiples of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, …
2. List a few multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, …
3. Compare the two lists. The numbers that appear in **both** lists are 6, 12, … – these are the common multiples of 2 and 3.
4. A common slip is to overlook the factor of **2** when checking the list; for example, choosing 9 (which is 3 × 3) misses the factor of 2 and therefore is not divisible by 2. The smallest common multiple is 6.

*Takeaway*: verify that each candidate number can be divided evenly by **both** original numbers.

*worked_example*

**Worked Example 2 – Common multiples of 4 and 6**

1. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, …
2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, …
3. The numbers appearing in **both** lists are 12, 24, … – these are the common multiples of 4 and 6.
4. A frequent mistake is to miss the factor of **3**; for instance, selecting 16 (which is 4 × 4) does not contain the factor 3, so 6 does not divide 16 evenly. The smallest common multiple is 12.

*Takeaway*: ensure the candidate number contains **all** prime factors of each original number.

### Candidate A
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

Recall that the **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each number in the set. A multiple is a number that can be divided by another number without a remainder, and when the same number works for two or more numbers it is a **common multiple** (see REF‑1).

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – LCM of 12 and 18 (prime‑factor method)**
1. Prime factorise each number:
   - 12 = 2² × 3¹
   - 18 = 2¹ × 3²
2. For each prime, keep the highest power that appears (2² and 3²).
3. Multiply those highest powers: LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
A common multiple is a multiple of two or more numbers (see REF‑2).

*practice*

**Practice Problems**
1. Find the LCM of 8 and 20.
   - Prime factors: 8 = 2³, 20 = 2² × 5¹
   - Highest powers: 2³ and 5¹
   - LCM = 2³ × 5¹ = 8 × 5 = **40**

2. Find the LCM of 15, 25 and 35.
   - Prime factors: 15 = 3¹ × 5¹, 25 = 5², 35 = 5¹ × 7¹
   - Highest powers: 3¹, 5², 7¹
   - LCM = 3¹ × 5² × 7¹ = 3 × 25 × 7 = **525**
