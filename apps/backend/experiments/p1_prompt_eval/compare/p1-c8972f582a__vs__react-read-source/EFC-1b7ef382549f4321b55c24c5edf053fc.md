### Candidate A
#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **multiple** of a whole number is a number that can be divided by that number without a remainder. A **common multiple** is a number that is a multiple of each of two (or more) numbers. For example, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because both 3 and 4 divide evenly into 12.

*worked_example*

**Worked Example:** Find the common multiples of 4 and 6.
1. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, …
2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, …
3. Numbers appearing in both lists are 12, 24, … – these are the common multiples of 4 and 6.
(The smallest common multiple, 12, would be the LCM, but here we only need the common multiples.)

#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **common multiple** of two or more whole numbers is a number that each of the numbers divides into exactly, leaving no remainder. For example, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because both 3 ÷ 12 and 4 ÷ 12 have no remainder. ### Candidate A
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each of the numbers.  A **common multiple** is a number that can be divided by each of the given numbers without a remainder (e.g., 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4).  This definition follows the textbook description of common multiples. (see REF‑1)

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – LCM of 12 and 18 (prime‑factor method)**
1. Prime factorise:
   - 12 = 2² × 3¹
   - 18 = 2¹ × 3²
2. Take the highest power of each distinct prime:
   - 2 → 2², 3 → 3²
3. Multiply the highest powers: LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
The method relies on the idea that the LCM must be a *common multiple* of the numbers, as defined in the textbook. (see REF‑2)

*reflection*

A common slip when finding an LCM is to **add the two numbers** instead of looking for a common multiple.  Adding 4 + 6 gives 10, but 10 is not divisible by both 4 and 6, so it cannot be the LCM.  Remember that the LCM must be a multiple of each original number, not their sum.  This aligns with the textbook definition of a common multiple. (see REF‑1, REF‑2)

*practice*

**Extra Practice (Stretch)**
1. Find the LCM of **24, 30, and 45**.
   - Prime factors: 24 = 2³ × 3¹, 30 = 2¹ × 3¹ × 5¹, 45 = 3² × 5¹
   - Highest powers: 2³, 3², 5¹
   - LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ = 8 × 9 × 5 = **360**
2. Find the LCM of **14, 21, and 28**.
   - Prime factors: 14 = 2¹ × 7¹, 21 = 3¹ × 7¹, 28 = 2² × 7¹
   - Highest powers: 2², 3¹, 7¹
   - LCM = 2² × 3¹ × 7¹ = 4 × 3 × 7 = **84**
These problems extend the usual two‑number practice and require handling three numbers and larger prime powers, providing a deeper challenge.  The idea of practicing common multiples comes from the textbook examples. (see REF‑3)
