## Objective lop01

### Candidate A
#### Identify common multiples of two or more whole numbers

*explanation*

A **multiple** of a whole number is a number that can be divided by that number without leaving a remainder. For example, 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so 12 is a multiple of 3.

A **common multiple** of two or more numbers is a number that is a multiple of **each** of those numbers. In other words, it must be divisible by every number in the set without remainder. This definition comes from the textbook and helps us see why, for instance, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because both 3 and 4 divide evenly into 12.

_References: REF-1, REF-2_

*worked_example*

**Worked Example:** Find the common multiples of 4 and 6.

1. **List multiples of 4** (stop after a few): 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, …
2. **List multiples of 6**: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, …
3. **Compare the two lists** and keep only the numbers that appear in **both** lists.
   - The first common number is **12** (both 4 and 6 divide it exactly).
   - The next common number is **24** (again divisible by both 4 and 6).
   - Continue if needed; any further common multiples will be multiples of the LCM (which in this case is 12).

**Why this works:** A number is a common multiple only if it passes the divisibility test for *each* original number. A common slip is forgetting to check one of the numbers, which can lead to picking a number that is only a multiple of one factor (e.g., 18 is a multiple of 6 but not of 4).

_Reference: REF-2_

*practice*

**Practice Problems** – Write down **two** common multiples for each pair. Use the hint below if needed.

1. **3 and 5:** ____, ____
2. **7 and 9:** ____, ____
3. **8 and 12:** ____, ____

**Hint:** A common multiple must be divisible by **both** numbers. Test each candidate by dividing it by each original number; both divisions should leave no remainder.

**Common slip to watch for:** Often students pick a number that is a multiple of only one of the pair (e.g., choosing 15 for 3 and 5 works, but choosing 9 for 7 and 9 would be correct while 14 would be wrong because it isn’t divisible by 9).

_Reference: REF-3_

A **multiple** of a whole number is a number that can be divided by that number without a remainder. For example, the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, … . A common slip here is forgetting to check that the candidate number divides evenly by **all** the given numbers; it must be a multiple of each one.

_Sources: REF-1, REF-2_

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – Finding common multiples of 2 and 3**

1. List a few multiples of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, …
2. List a few multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, …
3. Compare the two lists. The numbers that appear in **both** lists are 6, 12, … . These are the common multiples of 2 and 3.

**Addressing a typical misconception**: choosing 9 (a multiple of 3) as a common multiple overlooks the factor of 2. Because 9 ÷ 2 leaves a remainder, 9 is **not** a common multiple. The key is to verify that the candidate is divisible by **each** original number.

### Candidate A
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each of the numbers. This follows directly from the definition of a *common multiple* in the textbook. (see REF‑1, REF‑2)

*worked_example*

**Worked Example – LCM of 12 and 18**
1. Prime factorise:
   - 12 = 2² × 3¹
   - 18 = 2¹ × 3²
2. Take the highest power of each prime (2² and 3²).
3. Multiply: LCM = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
The same prime‑factor steps are described in the textbook. (see REF‑2)

*practice*

**Stretch Practice**
1. Find the LCM of **24, 30 and 45** using the prime‑factor method.
   - 24 = 2³ × 3¹
   - 30 = 2¹ × 3¹ × 5¹
   - 45 = 3² × 5¹
   - Highest powers: 2³, 3², 5¹
   - LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ = 8 × 9 × 5 = **360**.

2. **Textbook practice** – Find three common multiples of 5 and 6. (see REF‑3)
   - Example answers: 30, 60, 90.

Check your work by confirming each result is divisible by all the original numbers.

### Candidate B
#### Finding the LCM using the Prime‑Factor Method

*explanation*

The **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of a set of whole numbers is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each of the numbers. In other words, it is the first common multiple that appears when the multiples of the numbers are listed. A multiple is a number that can be divided by another number without a remainder, and a **common multiple** is a multiple of two or more numbers. (REF‑1, REF‑2)

*explanation*

**Worked Example – LCM of 12 and 18**
1. Prime factorise each number:
   - 12 = 2² × 3¹
   - 18 = 2¹ × 3²
2. Take the highest power of each prime:
   - 2 → 2², 3 → 3²
3. Multiply those highest powers: 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = **36**.
Thus, the LCM of 12 and 18 is 36.

*practice*

**Practice Problems**

   - Answer: **40**
2. Find the LCM of 15, 25 and 35.
   - Answer: **525**
