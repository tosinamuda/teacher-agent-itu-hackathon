## The revision pack

### Cylinders and Spheres: Knowing Their Shapes

You have already worked with shapes like cubes, cuboids, pyramids and cones. Now we meet two more solid (three-dimensional) shapes: the cylinder and the sphere. A solid shape is described by its faces, edges and vertices, so to identify a shape we count and name these parts carefully.

Start with the **cylinder**. Think of a tin of baked beans, a drinking straw, or a roll of toilet paper. A cylinder has:

- **Two circular faces** – one at the top and one at the bottom. These two circles are exactly the same size, and they lie flat and parallel to each other.
- **One curved surface** – the smooth, rounded wall that joins the two circles together.
- In total, a cylinder has **three faces**: two circular faces plus the one curved surface.
- A cylinder has **no vertices** – you will not find a sharp corner or point on it.
- A cylinder has **no straight edges** either, because the circular rims are curved, not straight.

So when someone asks how many faces a solid cylinder has, the answer is **three**: two circular faces and one curved surface.

Now think of a **sphere**. Picture a football, a marble, or an orange. A sphere is perfectly round – every point on its surface is the same distance from its centre. A sphere has:

- **Exactly one curved surface**, and no flat faces at all.
- **No edges** and **no vertices** – there is nothing sharp anywhere on it.

So the key difference between the two shapes is this: a cylinder has flat circular faces (two of them) and a curved surface, while a sphere is one smooth curved surface with no flat faces, no edges and no vertices.

**Worked example 1 – a cylinder**

Look at a solid cylinder. Let's count its parts step by step.

1. Hold the cylinder so you can see the top. There is a circular face. Flip it over – there is another circular face at the bottom. That gives **2 circular faces**.
2. Run your hand around the wall that joins the top to the bottom. That smooth rounded wall is the **curved surface** – **1 curved surface**.
3. Add them together: 2 circular faces + 1 curved surface = **3 faces** in total.
4. Finally, look for corners. A cylinder has **no vertices**, and it has **no straight edges**.

So a solid cylinder has **3 faces** (2 of them circular) and **no vertices**.

**Worked example 2 – a sphere**

Take a sphere, like a football.

1. Feel the whole surface. It is smooth and curved all over, with no flat part – so it has **1 curved surface** and **no flat faces**.
2. Look for any corner, edge or point. There are none – a sphere has **no vertices** and **no edges**.

So a sphere has just **1 face** (a curved one) and **no vertices**.

The two worked examples show the difference clearly: the cylinder uses two flat circular faces plus a curved surface, while the sphere is one smooth curved surface with nothing flat and nothing sharp.

### Finding the Volume of Cubes, Cuboids and Prisms

Volume tells you how much space a three-dimensional shape takes up. To measure it, think of filling the shape with little cubes. For a cuboid, you can picture it as rows of unit cubes.

A cuboid has three measurements: its length, its width (also called its breadth) and its height. To find its volume, you multiply all three together:

**Volume of a cuboid = length × width × height**

A cube is a special cuboid where the length, width and height are all equal. If the side of the cube is called \(s\), then its volume is:

**Volume of a cube = \(s \times s \times s = s^3\)**

So a cube of side 4 cm has volume \(4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64 \text{ cm}^3\).

A prism is a three-dimensional shape with two identical parallel faces (its bases) and sides that are rectangles or parallelograms. For any prism, you can find its volume by working out the area of the base first, then multiplying by the height of the prism:

**Volume of a prism = area of base × height**

A cuboid is itself a prism whose base is a rectangle, so this same rule works for it too: the area of its rectangular base is length × width, and multiplying by the height gives length × width × height.

Remember the units: if the measurements are in centimetres, the volume is in cubic centimetres, written cm³; if in metres, it is cubic metres, m³.

**Example 1: Volume of a cuboid**

Find the volume of a cuboid that is 8 cm long, 4 cm wide and 3 cm high.

Volume = length × width × height
= 8 × 4 × 3
= 32 × 3
= 96 cm³

**Example 2: Volume of a cube**

What is the volume of a cube of side 5 cm?

Volume = \(s^3 = 5 \times 5 \times 5\)
= 25 × 5
= 125 cm³

**Example 3: Volume of a prism**

A triangular prism has a base with area 10 cm² and a height of 7 cm. Find its volume.

Volume = area of base × height
= 10 × 7
= 70 cm³

**Practice**

1. Calculate the volume of a cuboid that is 8 cm long, 4 cm wide and 3 cm high.

2. Calculate the volume of a cuboid with dimensions 5 m by 2 m by 3 m.

3. What is the volume of a cube of 4 cm × 4 cm × 4 cm?

4. A rectangular tank is 6 m long and 2 m wide, and it holds 36 m³ of water. How deep is the water in the tank?
