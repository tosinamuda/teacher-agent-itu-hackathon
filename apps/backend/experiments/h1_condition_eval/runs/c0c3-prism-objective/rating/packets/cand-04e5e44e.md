## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is For: Collecting and Reading Data

Every day we are surrounded by facts and figures: how many goals a team scored, the marks in a test, the number of people in your street, the price of food in the market. These facts and figures, collected together, are called <b>data</b>. <b>Statistics</b> is the branch of mathematics that deals with collecting, organising, and reading data so that it can be understood and used.

<b>What is statistics for?</b> Statistics has several clear purposes. First, it helps us <b>collect</b> information in a careful and fair way, instead of guessing. Second, it helps us <b>organise</b> a large amount of information so that it is not a confusing jumble. Third, it helps us <b>compare</b> things — for example, which fruit is most liked by students. Fourth, it helps us <b>find patterns and trends</b>, such as whether rainfall increases in a certain month. Finally, it helps us <b>make decisions and predictions</b> — a shopkeeper uses data on what people buy to decide what to stock, and a government uses data on population to plan schools and hospitals.

<b>How is data collected?</b> Data can be gathered by <b>counting</b> (how many books on a shelf), by <b>measuring</b> (the heights of your classmates), by <b>asking questions</b> (a survey or questionnaire), by <b>observing</b> (watching traffic pass a road), or by reading existing <b>records</b> (a school register).

<b>How is data tabulated?</b> Once data is collected, it is put into a <b>table</b>. A table arranges the information neatly in rows and columns so that you can see at a glance how often each item occurs. The number of times an item appears is its <b>frequency</b>. A table that shows each item together with its frequency is called a <b>frequency table</b>.

<b>Worked example 1 — Collecting and tabulating data</b>

A class wants to know the favourite sport among 15 students. Each student names one sport. The answers collected are:

Football, Tennis, Football, Volleyball, Football, Tennis, Basketball, Football, Volleyball, Football, Tennis, Football, Basketball, Tennis, Football

<b>Step 1 — Collect.</b> The data is gathered by asking each student one question. The answers above are the raw data.

<b>Step 2 — Tally.</b> To organise it, we make a tally table. For each answer we draw one stroke. Every fifth stroke is drawn across the previous four to make counting easier (a group of five, like <b>IIII</b>).

Sport        | Tally  | Frequency
-------------|--------|----------
Football     | IIII II| 7
Tennis       | IIII   | 4
Volleyball   | II     | 2
Basketball   | II     | 2

<b>Step 3 — Interpret.</b> Reading the table, Football has the highest frequency (7), so Football is the most popular sport in the class. Basketball and Volleyball are equally popular, each with 2.

<b>Worked example 2 — Reading and comparing data in a table</b>

The table below shows the marks scored by students in a Mathematics test.

Marks      | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50
-----------|----|----|----|----|----
Frequency  | 3  | 5  | 8  | 6  | 2

Read the table to answer: (a) How many students scored 40? (b) Which mark was scored by most students? (c) How many students took the test?

(a) The frequency below 40 is 6, so 6 students scored 40.
(b) The largest frequency is 8, under the mark 30, so most students scored 30.
(c) Add all the frequencies: 3 + 5 + 8 + 6 + 2 = 24 students.

### Making Sense of Numbers with Tables

When we collect a lot of numbers, they can be hard to read. Statistics is the way we organise those numbers so they start to tell us something useful. One of the most helpful tools for doing this is a table.

A table takes rows of information and lines them up in columns, so you can see at a glance what is there. For example, suppose a class counted how many books each student read in a month. Instead of listing twenty separate numbers, we can put them in a table like this:

| Number of books read | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|----------------------|---|---|---|---|
| Number of students   | 4 | 7 | 6 | 3 |

To read a table, you ask yourself simple questions and let the numbers answer them.

Question: How many students read exactly 3 books? Look down the "Number of books read" row until you find 3. The number underneath it, 7, tells you that 7 students read 3 books.

Question: How many students were counted altogether? Add every number in the bottom row: 4 + 7 + 6 + 3 = 20 students.

Question: How many students read 4 or 5 books? Add the students in those two columns: 6 + 3 = 9 students.

So a table lets you compare groups, find totals, and answer questions quickly. The columns and rows are your signposts — always read the headings first so you know what each number is counting.

Example — Favourite fruits

A shop asked its customers which fruit they liked most and recorded the answers in this table:

| Fruit      | Apple | Banana | Mango | Orange |
|------------|-------|--------|-------|--------|
| Customers  | 12    | 8      | 15    | 5      |

Let's read a few facts from it.

Step 1: Which fruit was liked most?
Look for the biggest number in the "Customers" row. The biggest is 15, which sits under Mango. So mango was the favourite fruit.

Step 2: How many customers chose banana or orange?
Add the customers for those two fruits: 8 + 5 = 13 customers.

Step 3: How many customers answered altogether?
Add the whole "Customers" row: 12 + 8 + 15 + 5 = 40 customers.

Step 4: How many more customers chose apple than orange?
The apple column has 12 and the orange column has 5. The difference is 12 − 5 = 7 more customers chose apple.

Notice how each answer came straight from reading the numbers in the table. The headings told us what the numbers meant, and the numbers answered our questions.

To interpret a table confidently, use these three steps every time:

1. Read the headings. The row and column headings tell you what the numbers are counting. Without them, numbers have no meaning.

2. Find the right column or row. Put your finger on the heading you are interested in and follow it to the number you need.

3. Do the arithmetic the question asks for. Sometimes you just read one number. Sometimes you add columns together, or subtract one from another to find a difference.

A table is a summary: it takes a messy list of data and turns it into answers you can find. Once you can read a table, you can interpret statistical data — which simply means turning the numbers back into a clear description of what they show.
