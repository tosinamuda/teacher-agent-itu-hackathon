## The revision pack

### Properties of Cylinders and Spheres

Two common three-dimensional shapes are the cylinder and the sphere. You can tell each one apart by the faces, edges and vertices it has.

<b>The cylinder</b>

Look at the cylinder in fig-01. Think of a tin or a drum. A cylinder has a top and a bottom that are both circular, and a curved wall joining them.

Its properties are:

1. It has two circular faces and one curved face.
2. It has no vertex (no pointed corner).
3. It has two curved edges — the two circles where the curved face meets each circular face.
4. It has three faces altogether.

<b>The sphere</b>

Look at the sphere in fig-02. Think of a ball. Its whole surface is curved, with no flat parts at all.

Its properties are:

1. It is symmetrical from all directions — however you turn it, it looks the same.
2. It has no edge and no vertex.
3. It has one curved surface.

*[figure: A sphere drawn as a circle with an ellipse across its middle to show the curved surface.]*

*[figure: A cylinder drawn upright: an ellipse for the top face, two vertical sides falling from its ends, and a curve closing the base.]*

<b>Worked example</b>

Describe the faces, edges and vertices of a cylinder.

A cylinder has two circular faces and one curved face, so it has three faces altogether. Where each circular face meets the curved face, there is a curved edge, so it has two curved edges in total. Because a cylinder has no pointed corner, it has no vertex.

### Finding the Volume of a Cube, Cuboid and Prism

Volume measures how much space a solid takes up. For the solids you meet here, volume is found the same way every time: multiply the area of the base by the height. The only difference is how you work out that base area.

A cuboid is a box with rectangular faces. Its base is a rectangle, so:

Volume of a cuboid = area of base × height = length × breadth × height = l × b × h.

A cube is a special cuboid where length, breadth and height are all equal. If the side is l, then:

Volume of a cube = l × l × l = l³.

Worked example — finding the volume of a cuboid
Calculate the volume of a cuboid of length 3 cm, breadth 2 cm and height 8 cm.

Volume of cuboid = l × b × h
length = 3 cm, breadth = 2 cm, height = 8 cm
Volume = 3 cm × 2 cm × 8 cm = 48 cm³.

Worked example — finding the height of a cuboid
The volume of a cuboid is 24 cm³. Its base is 6 cm by 2 cm. Find its height.

Volume = l × b × h
24 cm³ = 6 cm × 2 cm × h
24 cm³ = 12 cm² × h
Divide both sides by 12 cm²:
24 cm³ ÷ 12 cm² = h
h = 2 cm.

Worked example — finding the volume of a cube
Calculate the volume of a cube of side 3 cm.

Volume of cube = l³
V = (3 cm)³ = 3 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm = 27 cm³.

Worked example — finding the volume of a prism
A prism has two identical ends. The volume of a prism is still:

Volume of prism = area of base × height.

Consider a triangular prism (fig-03) whose triangular end has a base of 5 m and a height of 3 m, running back 10 m.

First find the area of the triangular end (the base of the prism):
Area of triangle = ½ × b × h = ½ × 5 × 3 = 7.5 m².

Then multiply by the length of the prism:
Volume of prism = 7.5 m² × 10 m = 75 m³.

*[figure: A triangular prism lying on its rectangular face. The triangular end has a base of 5 m and a height of 3 m, and the prism runs back 10 m.]*

   The whole surface of a sphere is one curved surface with no flat parts, so there are no edges where flat faces meet and no pointed vertices.

1. 96 cm³

   Volume = l × b × h = 8 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm = 96 cm³.

2. 30 m³

   Volume = l × b × h = 5 m × 2 m × 3 m = 30 m³.

3. 64 cm³

   Volume of cube = l³ = 4 cm × 4 cm × 4 cm = 64 cm³.

4. 3 m

   Volume = l × b × h, so 36 m³ = 6 m × 2 m × h = 12 m² × h. Divide both sides by 12 m²: h = 36 m³ ÷ 12 m² = 3 m.
