### Plane Shapes: Their Types, Properties, Similarities and Differences

Plane shapes are shapes that lie flat on a surface, so they are also called two-dimensional shapes (2-D shapes). The plane shapes you will meet include the triangle, the square, the rectangle, the parallelogram, the trapezium, the circle and other polygons. A polygon is any closed shape that has three or more straight sides. Let us look at each family of plane shapes, its properties, and how the shapes are similar to or different from one another.

Look at the three triangles in fig-01: the isosceles, the scalene and the equilateral. Their names tell you how their sides compare.

- An <b>isosceles</b> triangle has two adjacent sides equal (the two slanted sides carry a single tick each). Its base angles are equal, and it has one line of symmetry.
- A <b>scalene</b> triangle has no side equal to another — each side carries a different number of ticks (one, two, three). No angle is equal to another, and it has no line of symmetry.
- An <b>equilateral</b> triangle has all three sides equal (a single tick on each side). All three angles are equal, each measuring $60^\circ$, and it has three lines of symmetry.

So the key differences come down to how many sides are equal: an equilateral has three equal sides, an isosceles has two, and a scalene has none.

*[figure: Isosceles: a single tick on each of the two slanted sides, an arc at each base angle. Scalene: one, two and three ticks on the three sides, an arc at each vertex. Equilateral: a single tick on each of the three sides, an arc at each vertex.]*

Now compare the square and the rectangle in fig-02. Each has a right-angle mark at every corner, so both have all angles equal, each measuring $90^\circ$, and both have two pairs of parallel sides. The difference lies in the sides.

- A <b>square</b> has all four sides equal (a single tick on every side). It has four lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other at a right angle.
- A <b>rectangle</b> has only two opposite sides equal — the two longer sides carry a single tick each and the two shorter sides a double tick each. It has two lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other but not at a right angle.

*[figure: Square: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the four sides. Rectangle: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the two longer sides and a double tick on each of the two shorter sides.]*

Finally, compare the parallelogram and the trapezium in fig-03.

- A <b>parallelogram</b> has two pairs of opposite sides equal and parallel (its two horizontal sides carry a single arrow and its two slanted sides a double arrow). Its opposite angles are equal, it has no line of symmetry, and its diagonals divide it into two equal triangles.
- A <b>trapezium</b> has only one pair of parallel sides (the two parallel sides carry a single arrow each, and one diagonal is drawn in). Its opposite angles are not equal, it has no line of symmetry, and its diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles.

The great difference between them is the number of parallel sides: a parallelogram has two pairs, a trapezium has only one pair.

*[figure: Parallelogram: a single arrow on each of the two horizontal sides, a double arrow on each of the two slanted sides. Trapezium: a single arrow on each of the two parallel sides, and one diagonal drawn in.]*

Polygons come in many forms, and each is named by its number of straight sides. The table below lists the common polygons, their number of sides, and how many triangles each can be divided into by drawing diagonals from one vertex to all the non-adjacent vertices.

| Name of polygon | Number of sides | Number of triangles ($n-2$) |
|---|---|---|
| Triangle | 3 | 1 |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 2 |
| Pentagon | 5 | 3 |
| Hexagon | 6 | 4 |
| Heptagon | 7 | 5 |
| Octagon | 8 | 6 |
| Nonagon | 9 | 7 |
| Decagon | 10 | 8 |
| Hendecagon (or undecagon) | 11 | 9 |
| Dodecagon | 12 | 10 |

The rule that produces the third column is simple: take the number of sides $n$ and subtract $2$. A hexagon has 6 sides, so it divides into $6-2=4$ triangles; a pentagon has 5 sides, so it divides into $5-2=3$ triangles.

<b>Worked example — triangles in a polygon</b>

How many triangles can a heptagon be divided into if you draw diagonals from one vertex to all the non-adjacent vertices?

<b>Solution.</b> A heptagon has 7 sides. Use the rule: number of triangles $= n-2 = 7-2 = 5$. So a heptagon divides into <b>5</b> triangles, which matches the table.

### Parts of a Circle

A circle is a plane shape with no corners: every point on its boundary is the same distance from its centre. The curved line that forms the round boundary of the circle is its **circumference**. fig-04 shows two labelled circles so you can see each part sitting on a real circle.

- The **centre** is the point in the middle of the circle.
- The **radius** (plural *radii*) is any straight line joining the centre to the circumference. In the second circle of fig-04 a radius is drawn from the centre out to the circumference, and the right-hand bounding line of the sector below is also a radius.
- A **chord** is any straight line joining two points on the circumference. The second circle in fig-04 has a chord drawn near the top.
- The **diameter** is any chord that passes through the centre of the circle. In the first circle of fig-04 the diameter is drawn straight across through the centre. Because it goes through the centre, it is the longest chord a circle can have, and it is twice as long as the radius.
- An **arc** is a curved part of the circumference. In the first circle of fig-04 a part of the circumference is marked and named the arc.

The figure also shows three more parts:
- A **segment** is the region of the circle cut off by a chord — the shaded part above the chord in the second circle.
- A **sector** is the region bounded by two radii and the arc between them — the shaded part below in the second circle, whose two straight sides are both radii.
- A **tangent** is a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point, as shown touching the right side of the second circle.

*[figure: Two labelled circles. The first has the diameter drawn across it and a radius from its centre to the circumference, with the arc and the circumference labelled. The second has a chord near the top with the segment above it shaded, a shaded sector below with its right-hand bounding line labelled Radius, and a tangent touching it on the right.]*

### Finding the Perimeter of Plane Shapes

The perimeter of a shape is the distance all the way round it. To find it you simply add the lengths of every side. For a straight-sided shape, this means adding each side once. For a circle, the perimeter has a special name — the circumference — and you find it using the radius (or diameter) and the number π (pi), which is about $\frac{22}{7}$ or 3.14.

Because some shapes have equal sides, you can often write a short formula instead of adding each side separately. Work through the examples below to see how each shape's perimeter is found.

<b>Example 1 — a square.</b> A square has four equal sides. In fig-05 each side is marked $L$ and measures 15cm. Its perimeter is four lengths added together:
$$L + L + L + L = 4L$$
$$= 15 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 4(15) = 60\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 2 — a rectangle.</b> A rectangle has two long sides $L$ and two short sides $b$. In fig-06, $L = 10\text{cm}$ and $b = 7\text{cm}$. Adding all four sides:
$$P = L + b + L + b = 2L + 2b = 2(L + b)$$
$$= 2(10 + 7) = 2(17) = 34\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 3 — a missing side.</b> Sometimes you are given the perimeter and one side and must find the other. Suppose a rectangle has perimeter 60cm and length 17cm (fig-07). Write the perimeter formula and solve for $b$:
$$P = 2L + 2b$$
$$60 = 2(17) + 2b$$
$$60 = 34 + 2b$$
Collect like terms:
$$60 - 34 = 2b$$
$$2b = 26$$
Divide both sides by 2:
$$b = 13\text{cm}.$$

*[figure: A rectangle with both long sides marked $L = 17$cm and both short sides marked $b$.]*

*[figure: A rectangle with both long sides marked $L$, the top one also 10cm, and both short sides marked $b$, the right-hand one also 7cm.]*

*[figure: A square, named *Square* at its left, with all four sides marked twice — $L$ and 15cm.]*

<b>Example 4 — a trapezium.</b> A trapezium has four sides, usually of different lengths. In fig-08 they are $a = 11\text{cm}$, $b = 9\text{cm}$, $c = 5\text{cm}$ and $d = 4\text{cm}$. The perimeter is all four added:
$$P = a + b + c + d$$
$$= 11 + 9 + 5 + 4 = 29\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 5 — a parallelogram.</b> A parallelogram has opposite sides equal: two of length $a$ and two of length $b$ (fig-09, where $a = 9\text{cm}$ and $b = 5\text{cm}$). So
$$P = a + a + b + b = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b)$$
$$= 2(9 + 5) = 2(14) = 28\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 6 — a regular hexagon.</b> A regular hexagon has six equal sides. In fig-10 each side is 5.2cm, so
$$P = 6 \times 5.2 = 31.2\text{cm}.$$

*[figure: A regular hexagon, named above the drawing, with each of its six sides marked 5.2cm.]*

*[figure: A parallelogram with a single arrow on each of its four sides: the two horizontal ones both lettered $a$, the lower also measured 9cm, and the two slanted ones both lettered $b$, the right-hand one also measured 5cm.]*

*[figure: A trapezium with each side lettered and measured: the top $c$, 5cm; the left $b$, 9cm; the right $d$, 4cm; the base $a$, 11cm.]*

<b>Example 7 — a circle.</b> The perimeter of a circle is its circumference, found with either formula:
$$C = 2\pi r \quad\text{or}\quad C = \pi d.$$
In fig-11 the radius is 7cm, using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$:
$$C = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 2 \times 22 = 44\text{cm}.$$
Using $\pi d$ instead: since $d = 2r = 2 \times 7 = 14\text{cm}$,
$$C = \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 22 \times 2 = 44\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 8 — a rope wound round a cylinder.</b> One turn round the cylinder is one circumference. With radius 25cm and $\pi = 3.14$,
$$C = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times 25 = 157\text{cm}.$$
Wound 50 times, the rope's total length is
$$157 \times 50 = 7850\text{cm}.$$

<b>Example 9 — the radius from the circumference.</b> If the circumference is 440cm, find $r$ ($\pi = \frac{22}{7}$):
$$440 = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r = \frac{44r}{7}.$$
Cross-multiply:
$$440 \times 7 = 44r \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3080 = 44r.$$
Divide both sides by 44:
$$r = \frac{3080}{44} = 70\text{cm}.$$

*[figure: A circle with a radius drawn from the centre to the right of the circumference, marked 7cm.]*

<b>Example 10 — a quarter circle.</b> The shape in fig-14 is made of two straight sides of 4.9cm each and a curved part that is one quarter of a full circle. Its perimeter is the two straight sides plus that curved part.

First find the curved part. It is $\frac{1}{4}$ of a full circumference:
$$\frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9$$
$$= \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{44}{7} \times 4.9 = \frac{11}{7} \times \frac{49}{10}$$
$$= \frac{11 \times 7}{10} = \frac{77}{10} = 7.7\text{cm}.$$

Now add the straight sides and the curved part:
$$P = 4.9 + 4.9 + 7.7 = 17.5\text{cm}.$$

*[figure: An upright cylinder with a rope wound round it, drawn as a series of turns spiralling down the body from the top face to the base.]*

*[figure: A circle with a radius drawn from the centre to the right of the circumference, marked $r$.]*
