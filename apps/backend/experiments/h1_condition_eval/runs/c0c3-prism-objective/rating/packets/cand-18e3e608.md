## The revision pack

### What Is Statistics and Why Do We Need It?

Every day we come across lots of information. The number of students in your class, how many bottles of water were sold at a shop in a week, the heights of your friends — these are all pieces of information. When we collect such facts together for a particular reason, we call them <strong>data</strong>. Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose.

<strong>Statistics</strong> is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data. So statistics is not just about gathering numbers — it is the whole way of handling information so that it becomes useful. Notice the five actions in that definition:

<ul>
<li><strong>Collecting</strong> — gathering the information you need.</li>
<li><strong>Organising</strong> — putting that information in order so it is easy to work with.</li>
<li><strong>Presenting</strong> — showing the information clearly, for example in a table.</li>
<li><strong>Analysing</strong> — studying the information to see what it tells you.</li>
<li><strong>Interpreting</strong> — explaining what the information means.</li>
</ul>

Why do we need statistics? It helps us to:

<ul>
<li><strong>Collect information</strong> — it shows us how to gather facts in an orderly way.</li>
<li><strong>Organise information</strong> — it lets us arrange the facts neatly so nothing is lost.</li>
<li><strong>Compare information</strong> — it lets us look at two sets of facts and see which is bigger, faster or more.</li>
<li><strong>Make decisions</strong> — with facts organised, we can choose wisely instead of guessing.</li>
<li><strong>Present information clearly</strong> — it lets us show others our findings in a way they can understand.</li>
</ul>

<strong>Worked example — seeing statistics in action</strong>

Suppose your class wants to know how many students come to school by bus, by bicycle and on foot. Here is how statistics helps at each step:

<ul>
<li><strong>Collect:</strong> You ask every student in the class how they travel and write down each answer.</li>
<li><strong>Organise:</strong> You group the answers — all the "bus" answers together, all the "bicycle" answers together, all the "foot" answers together.</li>
<li><strong>Present:</strong> You put the totals in a table so anyone can read them at a glance.</li>
<li><strong>Analyse and interpret:</strong> You look at the table and see that most students come by bus, so you can explain that to your class.</li>
</ul>

That last point is an example of a decision: seeing that most students travel by bus, your class might arrange a bus pick-up point. Statistics turned scattered answers into something useful.

### Collecting, Recording and Tabulating Data

Statistics is the study of information — called <strong>data</strong> — gathered about people, things or events. Before we can read and understand a table of numbers, the data must first be collected, and then set out neatly. There are four common ways to collect data:

1. <strong>Observation</strong> — you watch something happen and note what you see.
2. <strong>Counting</strong> — you count how many of each item there are.
3. <strong>Interview</strong> — you ask people questions face to face and record their answers.
4. <strong>Questionnaire</strong> — you give people a set of written questions to answer.

Whichever method you use, you end up with a list of items. For example, counting the vehicles passing a school might give you this list: <em>bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car</em>. A list like this is hard to read, so we use <strong>tally marks</strong> to record the data as it is collected.

<strong>Tally marks</strong> are short strokes used to record each item as it is counted. Every time you see one more of an item, you add a stroke. Strokes are grouped in fives: four upright strokes, then a fifth stroke drawn across them like <strong>||||/</strong>. This grouping makes it easy to count the total at a glance.

Let's record the vehicle data with tally marks. The data is: <em>bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car</em>.

- <strong>car</strong> appears 6 times → tally <strong>||||/ |</strong>, total 6
- <strong>bus</strong> appears 1 time → tally <strong>|</strong>, total 1
- <strong>lorry</strong> appears 2 times → tally <strong>||</strong>, total 2
- <strong>bicycle</strong> appears 3 times → tally <strong>|||</strong>, total 3

When the tally is finished, you can count up the strokes for each item and write the total in the last column. A table that shows how many times each item occurs in a set of data is called a <strong>frequency table</strong>. In a frequency table, the last column is the <strong>frequency</strong> — the number of times each item occurs.

Let's set the vehicle data out as a complete frequency table. First list each different vehicle in its own row, record the tally marks as you count, then write the total.

| Vehicles | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|
| car | \|\|\|\|\|/ \| | 6 |
| bus | \| | 1 |
| lorry | \|\| | 2 |
| bicycle | \|\|\| | 3 |

The frequency table shows at a glance that <strong>car</strong> is the most common vehicle (6), and <strong>bus</strong> the least common (1).

A frequency table does not always need a tally column — sometimes it just shows each item and its frequency. For example, a survey of students' favourite colours gives this frequency table:

| colour | frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

Here the most popular colour is Pink (30 students) and the least popular is Green (14 students). By setting data out in a table like this, you can read it quickly and answer questions about it easily.
