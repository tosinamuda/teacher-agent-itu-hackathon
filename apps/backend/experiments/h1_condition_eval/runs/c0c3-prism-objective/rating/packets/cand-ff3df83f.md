## The revision pack

### Cylinders and Spheres: Their Shapes and Properties

Look around your home and you will see objects with familiar 3D shapes. A tin of baked beans and a drinking glass are cylinders. A football and an orange are spheres. In this section we look closely at these two shapes and learn how to tell them apart by their properties.

<strong>Properties of a cylinder</strong>

A cylinder is a solid with two flat circular faces and one curved surface that joins them.

Let us count its parts carefully using a tin can:

• <strong>Faces:</strong> a cylinder has <strong>two faces</strong> — the circular top and the circular bottom. The curved part that wraps around the sides is not counted as a flat face; it is a curved surface.

• <strong>Edges:</strong> an edge is where two faces meet. The curved surface meets each circular face along a circle, so a cylinder has <strong>two curved edges</strong> (the rim of the top and the rim of the bottom).

• <strong>Vertices:</strong> a vertex is a corner, where edges meet at a point. A cylinder has <strong>no vertices</strong> — there is no sharp corner anywhere on it.

• <strong>Curved surface:</strong> the cylinder has <strong>one curved surface</strong> joining the two flat circular faces.

So a cylinder has 2 faces, 2 curved edges, 1 curved surface and 0 vertices.

<strong>Properties of a sphere</strong>

A sphere is a perfectly round solid, like a ball. Every point on its surface is the same distance from its centre.

• <strong>Faces:</strong> a sphere has <strong>no flat faces</strong>. Its whole surface is one curved surface.

• <strong>Edges:</strong> a sphere has <strong>no edges</strong>. There is no line where two surfaces meet.

• <strong>Vertices:</strong> a sphere has <strong>no vertices</strong>. There are no corners at all.

• <strong>Curved surface:</strong> a sphere has <strong>one curved surface</strong> that covers the whole shape.

So a sphere has 0 faces, 0 edges, 0 vertices, and 1 curved surface. Because it rolls in every direction, a sphere is sometimes called a "perfectly round" solid.

<strong>Comparing the two shapes</strong>

Here is a quick way to remember the difference. Both a cylinder and a sphere have one curved surface and no vertices. But a cylinder has two flat circular faces and two curved edges, while a sphere has no flat faces and no edges at all. So when you see a solid with a round flat top and a round flat bottom, it is a cylinder; when you see a solid that is round all over with no flat part, it is a sphere.

A useful check: imagine resting the shape on a table. A cylinder can stand still on its flat circular end, but a sphere will roll away — it has no flat surface to rest on.

<strong>Worked example 1</strong>

A can of soup has a flat circular top, a flat circular bottom, and a curved side that joins them. How many faces, edges and vertices does it have?

<strong>Think:</strong> the two flat circular parts are the two faces. The curved side is a curved surface, not a flat face. The curved surface meets the top along a circle and meets the bottom along another circle, giving two curved edges. There are no corners anywhere.

<strong>Write:</strong> faces = 2, curved edges = 2, vertices = 0.

<strong>Worked example 2</strong>

Classify each solid as a cylinder or a sphere: (a) a tennis ball, (b) a roll of sticky tape.

<strong>Think:</strong> a tennis ball is round all over, with no flat face and no edges — it is a sphere. A roll of sticky tape has a flat circular top and a flat circular bottom joined by a curved surface — it is a cylinder.

<strong>Write:</strong> (a) sphere, (b) cylinder.

3. A solid has two flat circular faces and one curved surface. What is the name of this solid?

4. Complete the table for a sphere: faces, edges, vertices, curved surfaces.

5. Name one object in your home shaped like a cylinder and one shaped like a sphere.

### Cylinders, Spheres, and Finding Volume

Let's meet two more three-dimensional shapes that you see all around you: a cylinder and a sphere.

A cylinder is the shape of a tin of baked beans, a drum, or a drinking glass. Its two ends are identical circles, and the curved surface joining them is smooth and straight along its length. A cylinder has two flat faces (the circular ends), one curved face, and no corners. The line that runs down the middle of the curved face, from the centre of one circle to the centre of the other, is the height (or length) of the cylinder, and the radius of either end is the radius of the cylinder.

A sphere is perfectly round, like a football, a globe, or a marble. It has just one curved face, with no flat faces, no edges and no corners. Every point on the surface of a sphere is exactly the same distance from its centre, and that distance is the radius of the sphere.

So remember: a cylinder has two flat circular faces and one curved face; a sphere has only one curved face and nothing flat at all.

Now we turn to finding how much space a three-dimensional shape takes up. That space is called its volume, and it is measured in cubic units, such as cubic centimetres (cm³).

A cube is a shape whose every face is a square, so all its edges are the same length. If each edge is s units long, then its volume is s × s × s, written as s³. For example, a cube with edges 3 cm long has volume 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 cm³.

A cuboid is like a box: its opposite faces are equal rectangles. If it has length l, breadth b and height h, then its volume is l × b × h. A cuboid 5 cm long, 4 cm wide and 2 cm high has volume 5 × 4 × 2 = 40 cm³.

A prism is a shape that has the same cross-section all the way through from one end to the other. The cross-section is the flat shape you see when you cut straight across the prism. A cuboid is one kind of prism, and so is a triangular prism, whose cross-section is a triangle. The volume of any prism is found the same way: the area of the cross-section multiplied by the length of the prism.

Volume of a prism = area of cross-section × length.

Worked example 1: Finding the volume of a cube
A cube has edges 4 cm long. Find its volume.

Step 1: A cube's volume is edge × edge × edge.
Step 2: 4 cm × 4 cm × 4 cm = 64 cm³.
Answer: The volume is 64 cm³.

Worked example 2: Finding the volume of a cuboid
A cuboid is 8 cm long, 5 cm broad and 3 cm high. Find its volume.

Step 1: A cuboid's volume is length × breadth × height.
Step 2: 8 cm × 5 cm × 3 cm = 120 cm³.
Answer: The volume is 120 cm³.

Worked example 3: Finding the volume of a triangular prism
A prism has a triangular cross-section with base 6 cm and height 4 cm, and the prism is 10 cm long. Find its volume.

Step 1: Find the area of the triangular cross-section. The area of a triangle is ½ × base × height, so ½ × 6 cm × 4 cm = 12 cm².
Step 2: Multiply the cross-section area by the length of the prism: 12 cm² × 10 cm = 120 cm³.
Answer: The volume is 120 cm³.

**Practice**

1. A cube has edges 2 cm long. Find its volume.

2. A cuboid is 6 cm long, 4 cm broad and 5 cm high. Find its volume.

3. A triangular prism has a triangular cross-section whose base is 8 cm and height is 5 cm. The prism is 9 cm long. Find its volume.

4. Name one shape from this lesson that has only a single curved face and no flat faces, and say how many faces, edges and corners a cylinder has.
