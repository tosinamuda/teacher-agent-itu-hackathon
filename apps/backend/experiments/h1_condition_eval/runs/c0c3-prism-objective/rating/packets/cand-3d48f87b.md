## The revision pack

### The Right Order for Algebraic Expressions

When you simplify an algebraic expression, you must deal with the operations in a fixed order. This order is often remembered by the word BODMAS:

- <b>B</b>rackets first
- <b>O</b>rder (powers such as squares and cubes)
- <b>D</b>ivision and <b>M</b>ultiplication (work from left to right)
- <b>A</b>ddition and <b>S</b>ubtraction (work from left to right)

The key idea is that you never just work from left to right. Brackets always come first, then powers, then multiply or divide, and only at the end do you add or subtract.

Multiplication and division are treated as equal in rank, so when you meet them together you simply work left to right. Addition and subtraction are also equal in rank, so when you meet them together you work left to right too.

Here is how the same steps work when the numbers are replaced by letters. For example, in the expression <b>3 + 2x</b>, the multiplication <b>2x</b> is done before the addition, because multiplication comes before addition. So <b>3 + 2x</b> already means "2 times x, then add 3" — never "3 + 2, then times x". The letter x stands for a number, and the ordinary order of operations still applies to it.

A useful habit is to rewrite each term clearly. A term like <b>4y ÷ 2</b> means "4 times y, divided by 2", so it simplifies to <b>2y</b> because 4 ÷ 2 = 2, then the y is carried along. Always work out the numbers first and keep the letter with its term.

<b>Worked example 1 — multiply and divide, then add</b>

Simplify <b>6x ÷ 2 + 4x</b>.

Step 1: Deal with the division first, because division comes before addition.
6x ÷ 2 = 3x

Step 2: Now add the terms.
3x + 4x = 7x

So 6x ÷ 2 + 4x = <b>7x</b>.

<b>Worked example 2 — brackets come first</b>

Simplify <b>3(x + 2y) − y</b>.

Step 1: Deal with the bracket first. Multiply everything inside the bracket by the 3 in front.
3(x + 2y) = 3x + 6y

Step 2: Now subtract the y that is outside the bracket.
3x + 6y − y = 3x + 5y

So 3(x + 2y) − y = <b>3x + 5y</b>.

<b>Worked example 3 — powers come before multiply</b>

Simplify <b>2x × x</b>. Here the expression has no brackets, but remember that x × x is x² (a power). Multiplication of the letters gives a power.

Step 1: Multiply the letters.
2x × x = 2x²

So 2x × x = <b>2x²</b>.

**Practice**

2. Simplify 2(3p + q) − 2q.

3. Simplify 8y ÷ 2 + y.

4. Simplify 5m + 2 + 2m, making sure to collect like terms in the right order.

### Brackets and Simplifying

Brackets are a way of grouping parts of an expression so we know what to deal with together. In algebra they do exactly the same job as in arithmetic: everything inside the brackets is treated as one group.

When a letter or number sits right in front of a bracket with no sign between them, it means <em>multiply</em>. For example, <strong>3(x + 2)</strong> means 3 lots of (x + 2), which is the same as 3 × x + 3 × 2.

To <strong>remove brackets</strong>, we multiply the term outside the bracket by every term inside it. This is sometimes called expanding the bracket. Once the brackets are gone, we often collect the like terms (the terms with the same letter) to finish the simplification.

Watch a full example of removing brackets and then simplifying.

<strong>Example:</strong> Simplify <strong>3(x + 2) + 4x</strong>.

Step 1 — Remove the bracket by multiplying 3 by each term inside:
3 × x + 3 × 2 = 3x + 6

Step 2 — Bring down the rest of the expression:
3x + 6 + 4x

Step 3 — Collect like terms (the x terms together, the number terms together):
3x + 4x + 6 = 7x + 6

So <strong>3(x + 2) + 4x = 7x + 6</strong>.

<strong>Worked Example 1 — Removing two brackets</strong>

Simplify <strong>2(x + 3) − 3(x − 1)</strong>.

Step 1 — Remove the first bracket:
2(x + 3) = 2x + 6

Step 2 — Remove the second bracket. The minus sign in front means we subtract 3 × (x − 1), so the −1 inside becomes −3 × −1 = +3:
−3(x − 1) = −3x + 3

Step 3 — Put it together:
2x + 6 − 3x + 3

Step 4 — Collect like terms:
2x − 3x + 6 + 3 = −x + 9

So <strong>2(x + 3) − 3(x − 1) = −x + 9</strong>.

<strong>Inserting brackets</strong> (also called factorising) is the reverse of removing them. We look at every term and find the highest common factor — the largest number and the largest letter power that divides into all the terms.

For example, in <strong>6x + 9</strong>, both 6x and 9 divide by 3. So we write the 3 outside the brackets and put the remaining parts inside:

6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3)

Check: 3 × 2x = 6x and 3 × 3 = 9, so it works.

<strong>Worked Example 2 — Inserting brackets</strong>

Factorise <strong>8y + 12</strong>.

Step 1 — Find the highest common factor of 8y and 12. The largest number that divides both 8 and 12 is 4.

Step 2 — Divide each term by 4:
8y ÷ 4 = 2y and 12 ÷ 4 = 3

Step 3 — Put the 4 outside the bracket:
8y + 12 = 4(2y + 3)

Step 4 — Check by expanding: 4 × 2y = 8y and 4 × 3 = 12. Correct.

<strong>Worked Example 3 — A bracket with a letter factor</strong>

Factorise <strong>5a + 10a²</strong>.

Both terms have a factor of 5 and also an a. The highest common factor is 5a.

5a ÷ 5a = 1 and 10a² ÷ 5a = 2a

So <strong>5a + 10a² = 5a(1 + 2a)</strong>.

**Practice**

1. Remove the brackets and simplify: 4(x + 5) + 2x

2. Remove the brackets and simplify: 5(y − 2) − 2(y + 1)

3. Insert brackets to factorise: 10x + 15

4. Insert brackets to factorise: 6m + 9m²

5. Remove the brackets and simplify: 2(3a + 4) + 3(a − 2)

### Order of Operations, Brackets, and Algebra in Words

When an algebraic expression contains several operations, you work through them in a fixed order. This order is the same one you use with numbers:

1. Do whatever is inside **brackets** first.
2. Then do **multiplication** and **division** from left to right.
3. Finally do **addition** and **subtraction** from left to right.

For example, in the expression \(5 + 3 \times 2x\), there are no brackets, so you multiply before you add:
\(5 + 3 \times 2x = 5 + 6x\).
The answer is \(5 + 6x\) — you cannot add 5 and 6x because they are not like terms.

If we put brackets in, the order changes. In \((5 + 3) \times 2x\), you work out the bracket first:
\((5 + 3) \times 2x = 8 \times 2x = 16x\).

So brackets can change the meaning of an expression completely. Always clear them out first.

To **remove a bracket**, multiply each term inside the bracket by what sits in front of it. This is called expanding the bracket.

\(3(2x + 4) = 3 \times 2x + 3 \times 4 = 6x + 12\)

When there is a minus sign in front of the bracket, it multiplies every term inside, so every sign flips:
\(- (3x - 5) = -3x + 5\)

Here is a longer simplification that puts everything together:
\[\n2(3x + 1) - (x - 4)\n\]
Remove both brackets:
\[\n= 6x + 2 - x + 4\n\]
Collect the like terms (the \(x\) terms, then the numbers):
\[\n= (6x - x) + (2 + 4) = 5x + 6\n\]

Always simplify fully so no like terms are left over.

Algebra is useful for solving **word problems**. To turn words into an algebraic expression, follow these steps:

1. Choose a letter to stand for the unknown amount.
2. Translate each phrase into a piece of algebra:
   - "three times a number" means \(3x\)
   - "five more than a number" means \(x + 5\)
   - "a number decreased by two" means \(x - 2\)
   - "the sum of a number and four" means \(x + 4\)
3. Combine the pieces using the order of operations and brackets.

Here is an example. "A number is multiplied by 3, then 7 is added to the result." The number is \(x\), so:
\[\n\text{result} = 3x + 7\n\]

Brackets matter in word problems too. "The sum of a number and 4, all multiplied by 2" is written as \(2(x + 4)\), not \(2x + 4\). The words "the sum of ... all multiplied by" tell you to add first, so the addition goes inside the bracket.

Worked example 1 — Order of operations
Simplify \(6 + 4 \times 3a\).

There are no brackets, so multiply before adding:
\(4 \times 3a = 12a\).
Then \(6 + 12a\).
\(6\) and \(12a\) are not like terms, so the answer is \(6 + 12a\).

Worked example 2 — Removing brackets
Simplify \(5(2x - 3) - 2(x + 4)\).

Expand the first bracket: \(5(2x - 3) = 10x - 15\).
Expand the second: \(-2(x + 4) = -2x - 8\).
Put them together:
\[\n10x - 15 - 2x - 8\n\]
Collect like terms:
\[\n(10x - 2x) + (-15 - 8) = 8x - 23\n\]
Answer: \(8x - 23\).

Worked example 3 — Word problem
"Twice the sum of a number and 5" — write this as an expression and simplify it.

Let the number be \(x\).
"The sum of a number and 5" is \(x + 5\).
"Twice" means multiply by 2, so the whole sum goes inside a bracket:
\[\n2(x + 5)\n\]
Expand:
\[\n2x + 10\n\]
So the expression is \(2x + 10\).

**Practice**

1. Simplify: \(9 + 2 \times 4x\)

2. Remove the brackets and simplify: \(3(2x + 1) + (x - 5)\)

3. A number \(x\) is increased by 3, and then the result is multiplied by 4. Write this as an algebraic expression and simplify it.

4. The difference between a number \(y\) and 2 is multiplied by 5. Write this as an algebraic expression and simplify it.

4. 3m(2 + 3m)

   Both terms share a factor of 3 and of m, so the highest common factor is 3m. Divide: 6m ÷ 3m = 2 and 9m² ÷ 3m = 3m. Write: 6m + 9m² = 3m(2 + 3m). Check by expanding: 3m × 2 = 6m and 3m × 3m = 9m².

   Remove the first bracket: 2(3a + 4) = 6a + 8. Remove the second: 3(a − 2) = 3a − 6. Together: 6a + 8 + 3a − 6. Collect like terms: 6a + 3a + 8 − 6 = 9a + 2.
