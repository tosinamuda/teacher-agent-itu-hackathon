## The revision pack

### Cubes and Cuboids: Faces, Edges and Vertices

In this section you will learn to pick out the properties of a cube and a cuboid. You already know that a cube and a cuboid are different — here we will look at exactly what makes each one what it is.

Every solid shape can be described by three kinds of parts:
- A **face** is a flat surface of the shape.
- An **edge** is a line where two faces meet.
- A **vertex** (plural **vertices**) is a corner where edges meet.

Now let us examine the cube.

**The cube**

A cube has 6 faces. Every face is a square, and all six squares are exactly the same size. This is what makes a cube a cube — every face is an identical square.

A cube has 12 edges. All 12 edges are the same length, because every face is the same square.

A cube has 8 vertices. Each corner of the cube is a vertex, and there are 8 of them (4 on top and 4 at the bottom).

So a cube has:
- 6 faces (all squares, all equal)
- 12 edges (all equal)
- 8 vertices

**The cuboid**

A cuboid also has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices — the same numbers as a cube. So what is the difference?

The difference is the shape of the faces. A cuboid's faces are **rectangles**, not squares. Usually the faces come in matching pairs: the two faces at the front and back are the same, the two at the sides are the same, and the two at the top and bottom are the same. The edges are not all equal — the cuboid has a length, a breadth and a height, and each is a different size.

So a cuboid has:
- 6 faces (all rectangles, in matching pairs)
- 12 edges (not all equal — length, breadth and height)
- 8 vertices

The handy way to remember the numbers: a cube and a cuboid both have **6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices**. They differ only in the shape of their faces — squares for the cube, rectangles for the cuboid.

**Worked example: reading the properties off a cuboid**

Think of a cuboid like a shoe box. Count its parts.

- **Faces:** a shoe box has a top, a bottom, a front, a back, and two sides — that is 6 faces. The front and back are the same rectangle, the two sides are the same rectangle, and the top and bottom are the same rectangle.
- **Edges:** every face is a rectangle with 4 sides, but each edge is shared by two faces. Count them by going around: 4 edges around the top, 4 around the bottom, and 4 joining the top to the bottom. That gives 12 edges in total.
- **Vertices:** there are 4 corners on the top and 4 on the bottom — 8 vertices in total.

So the cuboid has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices, exactly like a cube. The only difference is that its faces are rectangles (in matching pairs), and its edges are not all equal.

**Practice**

1. How many vertices has a cuboid?

2. How many edges has a cube?

3. A cube and a cuboid both have the same number of faces, edges and vertices. Write down those three numbers, and then state the one thing that makes a cube different from a cuboid.

### Pyramids and Cones: Meeting Their Parts

A pyramid and a cone both taper up to a single point, but their bases are different, and that changes what you can count on them.

Start with a pyramid. A pyramid has a flat base that is a polygon — a triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon, and so on. From each side of that base rises a triangular face, and all those triangular faces lean in to meet at one point on top, called the apex. So a pyramid is named by the shape of its base: a pyramid with a square base is a square-based pyramid, one with a hexagonal base is a hexagonal-based pyramid.

You can count the parts of any pyramid from its base:
- Faces: the base plus one triangular face for every side of the base. A square-based pyramid has 1 square base + 4 triangles = 5 faces.
- Vertices: the apex plus one corner for each corner of the base. A square-based pyramid has 1 + 4 = 5 vertices.
- Edges: the edges around the base, plus one edge from each base corner up to the apex. A square-based pyramid has 4 + 4 = 8 edges.

A special pyramid is the tetrahedron — a triangular-based pyramid. Its base is a triangle, so it has 1 + 3 = 4 faces, 1 + 3 = 4 vertices, and 3 + 3 = 6 edges.

A cone is different from a pyramid because its base is a circle, not a polygon. A cone has:
- One circular base.
- One curved surface that sweeps from the rim of the circle up to the apex — the pointed tip.
- One vertex, the apex itself.
- One edge — the circular rim where the curved surface meets the base.

So the quickest difference to remember: a pyramid is built from flat faces on a polygon base, while a cone is built from one flat circular base and one curved surface.

Worked example — a square-based pyramid

Take a pyramid whose base is a square. List its faces, vertices and edges.

The base is a square, which has 4 sides. The pyramid has that 1 square base, plus 1 triangular face rising from each of the 4 sides, so the faces are 1 + 4 = 5.

The vertices are the apex at the top plus the 4 corners of the square base: 1 + 4 = 5.

The edges are the 4 edges around the square base plus the 4 edges joining each base corner to the apex: 4 + 4 = 8.

So a square-based pyramid has 5 faces, 5 vertices and 8 edges.

**Practice**

1. Draw (or picture) a hexagonal-based pyramid. List the number of its faces, vertices and edges.

2. How many vertices does a tetrahedron have?

3. A cone has a curved surface and a circular base. How many vertices and how many edges does a cone have?
