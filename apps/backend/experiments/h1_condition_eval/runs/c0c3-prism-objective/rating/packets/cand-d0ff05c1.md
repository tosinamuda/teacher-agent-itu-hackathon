## The revision pack

### Why We Use Statistics

Every day you are surrounded by numbers. The number of students in your class, the scores in a test, the number of bottles in a crate, the ages of people in your family — all of these are pieces of information. On their own, scattered here and there, they are not very useful. But when you collect them, organise them and make sense of them, they become powerful. That is what statistics is about.

<b>Statistics</b> is the method of collecting and organising information so that it can be understood and used. It helps us answer questions like: How many? How many more? How many altogether? What is the most common?

Why do we need statistics? Because raw information, on its own, tells us very little. Once we collect it and put it in order, we can see what is happening, compare things, and make good decisions. Suppose a shopkeeper wants to know which drink sells best. If he just watches customers, he might guess. But if he counts and organises the sales in a table, he knows for certain which one to order more of. That is the purpose of statistics: to turn scattered information into something clear and useful.

Statistics is made of two main actions: <b>collecting</b> information and <b>organising</b> it.

<b>Collecting</b> means gathering the information from where it is. You might count the number of girls and boys in your class, or ask everyone their favourite colour.

<b>Organising</b> means putting the collected information in a neat, ordered form, usually in a table, so that it is easy to read.

Watch how collecting and organising work together. A teacher wants to know how many students are in each of two arms of Class 1. She counts: 24 students in 1A and 27 students in 1B. She has collected the information.

Now she organises it in a table:

| Class | Number of students |
|-------|--------------------|
| 1A    | 24                 |
| 1B    | 27                 |

Now the information is clear at a glance. You can see at once that 1B has more students than 1A, and you can add to find that together they have 24 + 27 = 51 students. That is the whole point of statistics — collecting the facts and organising them so the answers become obvious.

Let's work through collecting and organising information together.

<u>Example.</u> A group of friends wants to know how many red, blue and green pencils they have altogether. Amina counts and finds 8 red pencils, 5 blue pencils and 7 green pencils.

First, the information is <b>collected</b>: Amina has counted 8 red, 5 blue and 7 green.

Next, it is <b>organised</b> in a table so that it is easy to read:

| Colour of pencil | Number of pencils |
|------------------|-------------------|
| Red              | 8                 |
| Blue             | 5                 |
| Green            | 7                 |

Now the organised table lets us answer questions easily. How many pencils are there altogether? Add the column:

8 + 5 + 7 = 20

So there are 20 pencils in total. The table also shows that red pencils are the most common (8) and blue pencils are the fewest (5). This is exactly why we collect and organise information: scattered counts become a clear picture.

### Organising Data with Tally Marks and Frequency Tables

Raw data on its own is hard to read. If you are asked how many of your classmates are 12 years old, you do not want to count through a long, jumbled list every time. So we organise data into a table. Two tools help us do that: tally marks and a frequency table.

<b>Tally marks</b> are a quick way to count how many times each value appears. You draw one stroke for each item. When you reach the fifth, you draw it across the four like this: <b>𝍸</b> (four strokes, then the fifth crossing them). That makes groups of five, so you can count them in fives at the end.

A <b>frequency table</b> sets out each value together with the number of times it appears. The table has columns for the value, the tally marks, and the frequency (the count). Once the table is built, you can answer questions about the data just by reading the table.

<b>Worked example.</b> A class was asked how many pets each student owns, and these were the answers:

3, 1, 2, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2, 0, 3, 2, 1, 2

Let us build a frequency table.

<b>Step 1 — List the values.</b> The values that appear are 0, 1, 2 and 3. Write them in a column in order, smallest first.

<b>Step 2 — Tally.</b> Go through the list one answer at a time and make a tally mark beside that value:
- 0 appears twice: <b>𝍇</b>
- 1 appears three times: <b>𝍇</b> (three strokes)
- 2 appears five times: <b>𝍸</b>
- 3 appears three times: <b>𝍇</b>

<b>Step 3 — Count the tally marks.</b> Remember that a crossed group <b>𝍸</b> counts as five. So the frequencies are: 0 has 2, 1 has 3, 2 has 5, 3 has 3.

<b>Step 4 — Write the frequency table.</b>

| Number of pets | Tally | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 𝍇 | 2 |
| 1 | 𝍇 | 3 |
| 2 | 𝍸 | 5 |
| 3 | 𝍇 | 3 |

Now we can read answers straight from the table. How many students own 2 pets? The frequency for 2 is <b>5</b>. How many students answered altogether? Add the frequencies: 2 + 3 + 5 + 3 = <b>13</b> students.
