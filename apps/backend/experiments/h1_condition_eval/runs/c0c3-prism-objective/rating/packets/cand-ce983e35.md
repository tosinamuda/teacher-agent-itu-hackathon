### Getting to Know Plane Shapes

A <strong>plane shape</strong> is a flat shape that lies on a single level surface. You can think of it as a shape you could trace around the edge of, because it only has length and width — it has no height or thickness. The outside of the shape is its <strong>boundary</strong>, and the space inside that boundary is its <strong>area</strong>.

Plane shapes are grouped mainly by how many straight sides they have, and shapes with straight sides are called <strong>polygons</strong>. A polygon with three sides is a <strong>triangle</strong>, one with four sides is a <strong>quadrilateral</strong>, five sides a <strong>pentagon</strong>, six sides a <strong>hexagon</strong>, and so on. A shape with a curved boundary and no straight sides, like a circle, is <em>not</em> a polygon.

Here are the most common plane shapes and the properties that describe them:

<strong>Triangle</strong> — a polygon with exactly 3 sides and 3 corners (called <em>vertices</em>). The three sides may be equal or unequal, which gives different special triangles.

<strong>Square</strong> — a quadrilateral with 4 equal sides and 4 right angles (each angle is 90°). It is a special rectangle, because it has all the properties of a rectangle and one more: all its sides are equal.

<strong>Rectangle</strong> — a quadrilateral with 4 right angles. Its two opposite sides are equal and parallel, but the four sides are not all equal (unlike the square).

<strong>Parallelogram</strong> — a quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel, equal sides. Its opposite angles are equal, but its angles are not necessarily right angles.

<strong>Trapezium</strong> — a quadrilateral with only one pair of parallel sides. (Some books call this shape a <em>trapezoid</em>.)

<strong>Circle</strong> — a round plane shape with a curved boundary. It has no straight sides, no corners and no vertices. Every point on its boundary is the same distance from its centre.

When you compare shapes, you look for their <strong>similarities</strong> (things they have in common) and their <strong>differences</strong> (things that set them apart). Here is how to talk about them clearly.

<strong>Similarities among quadrilaterals.</strong> A square, rectangle, parallelogram and trapezium are all quadrilaterals, so each one has <em>4 sides, 4 corners and 4 angles</em>. That is their biggest similarity.

<strong>Differences between a square and a rectangle.</strong> Both have 4 right angles, so they are similar there. But a square has <em>all four sides equal</em>, while a rectangle has only its <em>opposite</em> sides equal. So every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square.

<strong>Differences between a parallelogram and a trapezium.</strong> A parallelogram has <em>two</em> pairs of parallel sides; a trapezium has only <em>one</em> pair. That single difference tells them apart.

<strong>How a circle differs from all polygons.</strong> A circle has a curved boundary and no straight sides and no corners, while every polygon has straight sides and corners. So the circle is the odd one out among the common plane shapes.

<strong>Worked example 1 — Naming a shape from its properties</strong>

A shape has 4 straight sides, exactly one pair of those sides are parallel, and none of its angles are right angles. What is the shape?

Look at each clue. It has 4 sides, so it is a quadrilateral. Only one pair of sides is parallel, which is the defining property of a <em>trapezium</em> — a parallelogram would have two pairs of parallel sides. The clue about no right angles is consistent, so the shape is a <strong>trapezium</strong>.

<strong>Worked example 2 — Spotting a similarity and a difference</strong>

Compare a square and a parallelogram.

Similarity: Both are quadrilaterals, so each has 4 sides and 4 corners.
Difference: A square has 4 right angles and all sides equal; a parallelogram has no right angles (in general) and only its opposite sides are equal. A square is a special kind of parallelogram because its opposite sides are parallel, but a parallelogram is not a square.

<strong>Worked example 3 — Telling a polygon from a non-polygon</strong>

Which of these is not a polygon: pentagon, triangle, circle, hexagon?

A polygon is made of straight sides and has corners. The pentagon, triangle and hexagon are all polygons. The circle has a curved boundary and no corners, so it is <em>not</em> a polygon.

### The Parts of a Circle

A circle is a special plane shape in which every point on its rim is the same distance from one fixed point at its middle. Because of this, a circle has its own set of named parts. Learning these names lets you describe circles precisely and get ready to measure them.

Here are the main parts of a circle and what each one means.

<strong>The centre</strong> is the fixed point exactly in the middle of the circle. Every other part of the circle is named in relation to it. We often label it with a letter, such as O.

<strong>The circumference</strong> is the distance all the way around the circle — it is the circle's outer boundary, the curved rim itself.

<strong>The radius</strong> (plural: radii) is any straight line that joins the centre to a point on the circumference. Every radius of a circle has the same length. If you double the radius, you get the diameter.

<strong>The diameter</strong> is a straight line that passes through the centre and touches the circumference at both ends. The diameter is twice the length of the radius, and every diameter divides the circle into two equal halves.

<strong>A chord</strong> is a straight line that joins two points on the circumference. The diameter is the longest possible chord, because it is the only chord that passes through the centre.

<strong>An arc</strong> is part of the circumference. A small part is called a minor arc, and the larger remaining part is called a major arc.

<strong>A sector</strong> is the region of the circle "sliced" between two radii, shaped like a slice of cake, with an arc as its curved edge.

<strong>A segment</strong> is the region between a chord and the arc it cuts off.

<strong>A semicircle</strong> is exactly half of a circle, bounded by a diameter and the arc it cuts.

<strong>A quadrant</strong> is a quarter of a circle, bounded by two perpendicular radii and the quarter-arc between them.

A quick way to remember the relationships: the radius reaches from the centre to the rim, the diameter reaches across the circle through the centre (twice the radius), and the circumference is the whole way around.

<strong>Worked example 1 — Naming the parts</strong>

Imagine a circle with its centre marked O. A line runs from O to the rim and is labelled r. Another line runs right across the circle through O, touching the rim at both ends, and is labelled d.

What are the lines r and d called, and how are their lengths related?

<em>Solution.</em> The line r joins the centre O to the circumference, so it is a radius. The line d passes through the centre and touches the circumference at both ends, so it is a diameter. The diameter is twice the radius, so d = 2r. Equally, the radius is half the diameter: r = d ÷ 2.

<strong>Worked example 2 — Spotting the diameter</strong>

A chord of a circle is 6 cm long and passes through the centre of the circle. What kind of chord is it, and what is the radius of the circle?

<em>Solution.</em> A chord that passes through the centre is a diameter. Since the diameter is 6 cm, the radius is half of it: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 cm.

<strong>Worked example 3 — Relating radius and diameter</strong>

The radius of a circle is 7 cm. Find the length of its diameter.

<em>Solution.</em> The diameter is twice the radius: 2 × 7 = 14 cm.

**Practice**

3. A straight line joins two points on the circumference of a circle but does not pass through the centre. What is this line called, and what is the name of the longest possible line of this kind?

### Finding Perimeter Around Plane Shapes

The perimeter of a plane shape is the total distance all the way around its outside edge. Think of it as the length of fencing you would need to go right around the shape. Because it is a length, perimeter is always measured in units of length such as centimetres (cm), metres (m) or kilometres (km).

You find the perimeter by adding up the lengths of every side of the shape. For some special shapes, there is a quicker way, because some sides are equal.

Let us meet the shapes you will work with.

A square has four equal sides. If one side is called <b>s</b>, then the perimeter is <b>s + s + s + s = 4s</b>. So <b>Perimeter of a square = 4 × side</b>.

A rectangle has two pairs of equal sides: two lengths and two breadths (widths). If the length is <b>L</b> and the breadth is <b>B</b>, then <b>Perimeter = 2 × (L + B)</b>, because the opposite sides match.

A regular polygon is any shape whose sides are all equal and whose angles are all equal — for example a regular pentagon (5 sides), a regular hexagon (6 sides) or a regular octagon (8 sides). If a regular polygon has <b>n</b> equal sides and each side is <b>s</b> long, then <b>Perimeter = n × s</b>.

A trapezium has four sides, but only one pair of opposite sides is parallel. There is no shortcut, so you just add up all four sides: <b>Perimeter = a + b + c + d</b>.

A parallelogram has two pairs of equal, opposite sides. If the two unequal side lengths are <b>a</b> and <b>b</b>, then <b>Perimeter = 2 × (a + b)</b>, just like a rectangle.

A circle is different. Its perimeter is called the <b>circumference</b>. A circle does not have straight sides, so it needs its own rule. The circumference depends on how far it is across the middle (the diameter, <b>d</b>) or from the middle to the edge (the radius, <b>r</b>, which is half the diameter). Using the number <b>π</b> (about 3.14), <b>Circumference = π × d = 2 × π × r</b>.

<b>Worked example 1 — a square and a rectangle</b><br>A square has sides of 6 cm. Its perimeter is 4 × 6 cm = 24 cm. A rectangle is 10 cm long and 4 cm broad. Its perimeter is 2 × (10 + 4) = 2 × 14 = 28 cm.

<b>Worked example 2 — a regular polygon</b><br>A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides, each 5 cm. Perimeter = n × s = 6 × 5 = 30 cm.

<b>Worked example 3 — a trapezium and a parallelogram</b><br>A trapezium has sides 3 cm, 5 cm, 4 cm and 6 cm. Perimeter = 3 + 5 + 4 + 6 = 18 cm. A parallelogram has sides 7 cm and 3 cm (with the opposite sides matching). Perimeter = 2 × (7 + 3) = 2 × 10 = 20 cm.

<b>Worked example 4 — a circle</b><br>A circle has a diameter of 10 cm. Circumference = π × d = 3.14 × 10 = 31.4 cm. If instead we are given a radius of 4 m, then Circumference = 2 × π × r = 2 × 3.14 × 4 = 25.12 m.

**Practice**

2. A rectangle is 12 cm long and 7 cm broad. Find its perimeter.

3. A regular pentagon has sides of 8 cm each. What is its perimeter?

4. A trapezium has sides of 4 cm, 6 cm, 5 cm and 7 cm. Find its perimeter.

5. A parallelogram has sides of 9 cm and 5 cm. Find its perimeter.

6. A circle has a radius of 7 cm. Use π = 3.14. What is its circumference?
