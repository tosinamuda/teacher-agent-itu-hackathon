## The revision pack

### The Right Order: Working Through Algebraic Expressions

An algebraic expression is a sentence of mathematics built from letters (like x and y) and numbers, joined by operations such as +, −, × and ÷. For example, <strong>2x + 3</strong> and <strong>4(x − 1) + 5</strong> are both algebraic expressions.

When an expression has more than one operation, you cannot simply work from left to right. Everyone must get the same answer, so mathematicians agree on one fixed order to follow. The order is often remembered with the word <strong>BODMAS</strong>:

• <strong>B</strong>rackets — work out anything inside brackets first
• <strong>O</strong>rders — powers, such as x², next
• <strong>D</strong>ivision and <strong>M</strong>ultiplication — from left to right
• <strong>A</strong>ddition and <strong>S</strong>ubtraction — from left to right, last

The same order is used in algebra as with plain numbers. The letters simply stand in for numbers, so they follow exactly the same rules. Whenever you see brackets, deal with them first — and remember that a number written next to a bracket, like <strong>3(x + 2)</strong>, means multiply everything inside by that number (this is called expanding the bracket).

<strong>Example 1 — Multiplication before addition</strong>
Simplify <strong>2 + 3 × 4x</strong>.

There is no bracket and no power here. BODMAS says multiplication comes before addition, so do the 3 × 4x first.
3 × 4x = 12x.
Now add: 2 + 12x.
So <strong>2 + 3 × 4x = 2 + 12x</strong>. We cannot add the 2 and the 12x together because one is a plain number and the other has an x, so the expression is now as simple as it can be.

<strong>Example 2 — Removing a bracket</strong>
Simplify <strong>4(2x − 3)</strong>.

The bracket is first, and the 4 outside multiplies both terms inside.
4 × 2x = 8x, and 4 × (−3) = −12.
So <strong>4(2x − 3) = 8x − 12</strong>.

<strong>Example 3 — Brackets then collecting like terms</strong>
Simplify <strong>3x + 2(x + 5) − 7</strong>.

Step 1 — the bracket: 2(x + 5) = 2x + 10.
The expression becomes 3x + 2x + 10 − 7.
Step 2 — collect the x-terms: 3x + 2x = 5x.
Step 3 — collect the numbers: 10 − 7 = 3.
So <strong>3x + 2(x + 5) − 7 = 5x + 3</strong>.

The order of operations also helps with <strong>word problems</strong>. A word problem describes a situation in sentences, and you turn it into an algebraic expression using a letter for the unknown. Read carefully, decide which operation each phrase points to (for example, "more than" means add, "times" means multiply, "the difference" means subtract), then simplify using BODMAS.

Here is a useful way to think about it: if a problem mentions a total built from separate parts, brackets often hold one part so it is dealt with before the rest of the expression.

<strong>Example 4 — A rectangle</strong>
A rectangle has length <strong>(x + 4) cm</strong> and width <strong>3 cm</strong>. Write a simplified expression for its perimeter.

The perimeter of a rectangle is twice the length plus twice the width:
Perimeter = 2(length + width) = 2((x + 4) + 3).
Work inside the bracket first: (x + 4) + 3 = x + 7.
Now expand: 2(x + 7) = 2x + 14.
So the perimeter is <strong>2x + 14 cm</strong>.

<strong>Example 5 — A shopping problem</strong>
A pencil costs x naira and a pen costs 5 naira more. Amina buys 3 pencils and 2 pens. Write a simplified expression for the total cost.

A pen costs x + 5 naira.
3 pencils cost 3 × x = 3x naira.
2 pens cost 2 × (x + 5) = 2x + 10 naira.
Total = 3x + 2x + 10 = 5x + 10 naira.
So the total cost is <strong>5x + 10 naira</strong>.

### Removing and Inserting Brackets

Brackets hold terms together, and often an expression is easier to work with once you have either taken the brackets away or grouped terms inside a new pair of brackets. Both moves follow the same simple idea: whatever sits right in front of a bracket multiplies every single term inside it.

<b>Removing a bracket</b>
When a letter or number stands in front of a bracket, multiply it by each term inside, then drop the brackets:
- <b>3(x + 2)</b> = 3·x + 3·2 = <b>3x + 6</b>
- <b>2(b − 5)</b> = 2·b − 2·5 = <b>2b − 10</b>

When a <b>minus sign</b> stands in front of a bracket, you change the sign of every term inside and then drop the brackets. Think of the minus as −1 multiplying each term:
- <b>−(a + 4)</b> = −a − 4
- <b>−(2y − 3)</b> = −2y + 3 &nbsp; (the −3 becomes +3)

<b>Inserting a bracket</b>
To put terms back into a bracket, find the largest factor that divides every term, write it in front of the bracket, and divide each term by it:
- <b>6x + 9</b>: 3 divides both terms, so 6x + 9 = <b>3(2x + 3)</b>.
- <b>8p − 4</b>: 4 divides both terms, so 8p − 4 = <b>4(2p − 1)</b>.

<b>Simplifying after removing brackets</b>
Once the brackets are gone, finish by collecting the like terms — add or subtract the terms that have the same letter (and the plain numbers on their own). For example, 3x + 6 + 4x − 4 becomes (3x + 4x) + (6 − 4) = 7x + 2. Work carefully, one sign at a time, and you will always end with a tidy expression.

<b>Example 1: Remove the brackets and simplify 4(x + 3) + 2(x − 1)</b>

Remove each bracket first, multiplying the number in front by every term inside:
4(x + 3) = 4x + 12 &nbsp; and &nbsp; 2(x − 1) = 2x − 2

So the expression becomes: 4x + 12 + 2x − 2

Collect the like terms. The x-terms: 4x + 2x = 6x. The plain numbers: 12 − 2 = 10.

Answer: <b>6x + 10</b>

<b>Example 2: Remove the brackets and simplify 5a − (2a + 3)</b>

The bracket is preceded by a minus sign, so change the sign of every term inside and drop the brackets:
−(2a + 3) = −2a − 3

The expression becomes: 5a − 2a − 3

Collect the like terms: 5a − 2a = 3a. The number −3 has no a to join, so it stays.

Answer: <b>3a − 3</b>

<b>Example 3: Insert a bracket to write 12m − 8 as a product</b>

Find the largest factor of both terms. 12m and 8 are both divisible by 4, so bring 4 out in front:
12m ÷ 4 = 3m &nbsp; and &nbsp; 8 ÷ 4 = 2

Answer: <b>4(3m − 2)</b>. Check by removing the bracket: 4·3m − 4·2 = 12m − 8, which matches.

### From Words to Algebra

A word problem gives you a story, and your job is to turn the story into algebra, work with the expression you build, and then answer the question the story is asking. You already know how to simplify algebraic terms by collecting like terms and applying the order of operations. Word problems simply ask you to spot which operation (add, subtract, multiply, divide) the words are describing, and to use a letter for the unknown amount.

The key moves are these:
1. Read the problem and decide what the unknown is. Give it a letter, such as <b>x</b> or <b>y</b>.
2. Write each sentence of the story as part of an algebraic expression.
3. Simplify the expression by collecting like terms.
4. Use the final answer to answer the original question.

Some phrases give you strong clues:
• "more than", "add", "sum of" → addition
• "less than", "difference", "take away" → subtraction
• "times", "product of", "twice", "three times" → multiplication
• "shared equally", "divided between" → division

Example 1 — One unknown.
A number <b>x</b> is increased by 5, and the result is then tripled. Write an expression for the final amount.

Step 1: The unknown is the number <b>x</b>.
Step 2: "increased by 5" means <b>x</b> + 5.
Step 3: "tripled" means multiply by 3, so put the whole thing in brackets: 3(<b>x</b> + 5).
Step 4: Expand the bracket: 3<b>x</b> + 15.

So the final expression is 3<b>x</b> + 15.

Example 2 — Two unknowns in one story.
Amina has <b>y</b> oranges. Her brother has 3 more than twice as many oranges as Amina. Write, in terms of <b>y</b>, how many oranges her brother has.

Step 1: Amina has <b>y</b> oranges.
Step 2: "twice as many as Amina" means 2<b>y</b>.
Step 3: "3 more than" that means add 3: 2<b>y</b> + 3.

So her brother has 2<b>y</b> + 3 oranges.

Example 3 — A word problem you simplify then evaluate.
A rectangle has length <b>x</b> cm. Its width is 2 cm less than its length.
(a) Write an expression for the perimeter of the rectangle.
(b) Find the perimeter when <b>x</b> = 6.

Step 1: Length = <b>x</b> cm. Width = 2 cm less than length, so width = <b>x</b> − 2.
Step 2: Perimeter = length + width + length + width = 2 × length + 2 × width.
So perimeter = 2<b>x</b> + 2(<b>x</b> − 2).
Step 3: Expand the bracket: 2<b>x</b> + 2<b>x</b> − 4 = 4<b>x</b> − 4.
Step 4: When <b>x</b> = 6, perimeter = 4(6) − 4 = 24 − 4 = 20 cm.

So (a) the perimeter is 4<b>x</b> − 4 cm, and (b) it is 20 cm when <b>x</b> = 6.

Notice how a single word problem can combine everything: building the expression from the words, then simplifying it by expanding brackets and collecting like terms, and sometimes putting in a value for the letter at the end. Keep your steps in the same order every time, and check that your final expression actually answers the question asked — not just the algebra you did.

## Check your work

**The Right Order: Working Through Algebraic Expressions**

1. 6 + 6x

   Multiplication comes before addition. 2 × 3x = 6x, then add the 6: 6 + 6x.

2. 13x − 12

   Expand the bracket first: 4(2x − 3) = 8x − 12. The expression becomes 8x − 12 + 5x. Collect like terms: 8x + 5x = 13x. So the answer is 13x − 12.

3. 5x + 3

   Bracket first: 2(x + 5) = 2x + 10. The expression becomes 3x + 2x + 10 − 7. Collect x-terms: 3x + 2x = 5x. Collect numbers: 10 − 7 = 3. Answer: 5x + 3.

4. 4x + 10 cm

   Perimeter = 2(length + width) = 2((2x + 1) + 4). Work inside the bracket: (2x + 1) + 4 = 2x + 5. Expand: 2(2x + 5) = 4x + 10. So the perimeter is 4x + 10 cm.

5. 3x + 2 kg

   Three times the box is 3 × x = 3x kg, and 2 kg more is added last. So the crate weighs 3x + 2 kg.

**Removing and Inserting Brackets**

1. 6x + 10

   4(x + 3) = 4x + 12 and 2(x − 1) = 2x − 2. The expression is 4x + 12 + 2x − 2. Collect like terms: 4x + 2x = 6x and 12 − 2 = 10, so the answer is 6x + 10.

2. 4a − 2

   The minus in front of the bracket changes every sign inside: −(3a + 2) = −3a − 2. So 7a − 3a − 2 = 4a − 2.

3. 3b + 14

   2(3b + 1) = 6b + 2 and −3(b − 4) = −3b + 12. The expression is 6b + 2 − 3b + 12. Collect like terms: 6b − 3b = 3b and 2 + 12 = 14, so the answer is 3b + 14.

4. 4(2x − 3)

   The largest factor of 8x and 12 is 4. Bring 4 out: 8x ÷ 4 = 2x and 12 ÷ 4 = 3, so 8x − 12 = 4(2x − 3). Check: 4·2x − 4·3 = 8x − 12.

5. 4x + 14

   Remove the brackets: 2(2x + 3) = 4x + 6 and 2(4) = 8. The expression is 4x + 6 + 8. Collect like terms: 4x stays, and 6 + 8 = 14, so the perimeter is 4x + 14.

**From Words to Algebra**

1. 4(<b>n</b> − 7) = 4<b>n</b> − 28

   Decreased by 7 means <b>n</b> − 7. Multiply the whole result by 4, so bracket it: 4(<b>n</b> − 7). Expand: 4<b>n</b> − 28.

2. 2<b>m</b> − 4 years old

   Twice Bala's age is 2<b>m</b>. Four years younger means subtract 4: 2<b>m</b> − 4.

3. (a) 4<b>y</b> + 6 cm; (b) 26 cm

   Length = <b>y</b>, width = <b>y</b> + 3. Perimeter = 2<b>y</b> + 2(<b>y</b> + 3) = 2<b>y</b> + 2<b>y</b> + 6 = 4<b>y</b> + 6. When <b>y</b> = 5: 4(5) + 6 = 20 + 6 = 26 cm.
