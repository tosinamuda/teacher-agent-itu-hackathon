## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is and What It Is For

When you count the pencils in your pencil case, or the number of learners in your class, you are dealing with information. That information — the facts or numbers you collect — is called <strong>data</strong>. Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose.

<strong>Statistics</strong> is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data. Let us look at each of those words:

- <strong>Collecting</strong> means gathering the facts or information you need.
- <strong>Organising</strong> means putting that information into some order, so it is easy to read.
- <strong>Presenting</strong> means showing the information clearly, for example in a table.
- <strong>Analysing</strong> means studying it carefully to see what it tells you.
- <strong>Interpreting</strong> means explaining what the information means.

So statistics is the whole process of working with data — from collecting it, right through to explaining what it shows.

Why do we bother with all of this? Statistics is important because it helps us to:

- <strong>Collect information</strong> — gather the facts we need in an orderly way.
- <strong>Organise information</strong> — arrange the facts so they are easy to manage and read.
- <strong>Compare information</strong> — put facts side by side and see how they are alike or different.
- <strong>Make decisions</strong> — use the facts to choose sensibly, rather than guessing.
- <strong>Present information clearly</strong> — show the facts so that other people can understand them at a glance.

In short, statistics gives us a reliable way to handle information, so that the choices we make are based on real facts.

Think about the facts of your own class. The number of boys and the number of girls are pieces of <strong>data</strong> — facts collected about your class. When you write them in a table so they are easy to read, you are <strong>organising</strong> the data. When you look at the two numbers and say which group is larger, you are <strong>comparing</strong> the information. And if you use that comparison to decide, say, how many chairs the class needs, you have used statistics to <strong>make a decision</strong>. One set of data can pass through every part of statistics in just a few steps.

### Collecting Data with Tally Marks and Building a Frequency Table

When we want to answer a question like "How many cars pass our school each morning?" we first have to gather the information. That gathered information is called **data**. Data can be collected in four main ways:

1. **Observation** — you watch and note down what you see.
2. **Counting** — you count things, such as the number of students present.
3. **Interview** — you ask people questions and record their answers.
4. **Questionnaire** — you hand out a set of written questions for people to answer.

Once the data is collected, it can be a jumbled list, which is hard to read. So we tidy it up using **tally marks** and a **frequency table**.

A **tally mark** is a single stroke (|) used to record one item. To make counting easy, every fifth item is marked by drawing a stroke across the previous four, like this: **||||**. A group of five tally marks is always drawn as four vertical strokes with one stroke across them. Each group stands for 5.

A **frequency table** is a table showing how many times each item occurs in a set of data. The number of times an item appears is called its **frequency**.

To build a frequency table, you go through your data one item at a time. Each time you meet an item, add a tally stroke next to it. When an item reaches five strokes, you close the group by drawing the fifth stroke across. At the end, count the strokes in each row and write that number as the total (frequency).

**Worked example — recording vehicles with tally marks**

Two students collected the same data about the vehicles that passed their school:

bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car

This jumbled list is hard to read, so let us record it with tally marks and show it in a table.

Work through the list one vehicle at a time and add a stroke each time:

- **car** appears 6 times. Its tally is one group of five plus one more: **|||| |**.
- **bus** appears 1 time. Its tally is **|**.
- **lorry** appears 2 times. Its tally is **||**.
- **bicycle** appears 3 times. Its tally is **|||**.
- **motor bike** appears 0 times, so it gets no tally marks.

Now we count the strokes in each row to find the total:

| Vehicles | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|
| car | |||| \| | 6 |
| bus | \| | 1 |
| lorry | \|\| | 2 |
| bicycle | \|\|\| | 3 |
| motor bike | | 0 |

This table shows at a glance which vehicle was most common (car, with 6) and which was least common (motor bike, with 0). The "Total" column is the frequency of each vehicle.

**Worked example — a frequency table of favourite colours**

A class of students was asked for their favourite colour. The results were counted and written as a frequency table:

| colour | frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

This table shows how many students chose each colour. The frequency of a colour is just the number of students who chose it. For example, the frequency of **Pink** is 30, which means 30 students said pink was their favourite. Reading the table, we can compare the colours at a glance: Blue (20) and Pink (30) were chosen more than Green (14).
