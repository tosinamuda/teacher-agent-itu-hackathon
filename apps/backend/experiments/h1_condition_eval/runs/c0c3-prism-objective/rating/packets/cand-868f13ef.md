## The revision pack

### Properties of Cubes and Cuboids

Three-dimensional shapes are shapes that have length, breadth and height. Because they have all three, they are also called solid shapes. A solid shape can be regular or irregular. Regular shapes have a tidy, fixed form — a cube of sugar, a can of drink and a match box are all regular shapes. Irregular shapes are not tidy — a stone or a tree is irregular.

In this section you will learn the properties of two regular solids: the cube and the cuboid.

Before you read the properties, settle three words that describe any solid:

- A <strong>face</strong> (or surface) is a flat or curved side of a solid.
- An <strong>edge</strong> is a line where two faces meet. It may be straight or curved.
- A <strong>vertex</strong> is a point corner where edges meet. The plural of vertex is vertices.

Look at fig-01. It shows a cube drawn as a see-through solid — two equal squares, one nearer to you and one farther away, with the four pairs of matching corners joined. Every face of a cube is an equal square.

A cube has these properties:

1. It is a solid shape.
2. It has 6 square faces.
3. It has 12 edges.
4. It has 8 vertices.

Count them on fig-01 if you like: six faces, twelve edges and eight vertices.

*[figure: A cube drawn as a see-through solid: two equal squares, the nearer one offset down and to the right of the farther one, with the four pairs of corresponding corners joined.]*

Now look at fig-02, a cuboid drawn the same way — wide and shallow. Three leader lines point to the top face, the nearer top-right vertex, and the nearer right-hand vertical edge, so you can see where the faces, vertices and edges sit.

A cuboid has these properties:

1. It has 6 faces.
2. It has 12 edges.
3. It has 8 vertices.

Notice the counts: the cube and the cuboid both have 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices. What makes a cube special is that all six of its faces are equal squares; a cuboid's faces can be rectangles, so it does not have to be the same on every side.

*[figure: A cuboid drawn the same way, wide and shallow, with three leader lines to the right naming the top face, the nearer top-right corner and the nearer right-hand vertical edge.]*

### Pyramids and Cones: Faces, Edges and Vertices

A pyramid gets its name from the shape of its base. Look at the triangular based pyramid in fig-03, the square based pyramid in fig-04, the pentagonal based pyramid in fig-05, and the rectangular based pyramid in fig-06. In every one, the sides rise from the base and meet at a single point at the top, called the apex. So a pyramid with a square base is called a square based pyramid, a pyramid with a triangular base is a triangular based pyramid, and so on. A triangular based pyramid has a special name of its own — a tetrahedron.

*[figure: A rectangular based pyramid, drawn like the square one but on a base wider than it is tall.]*

*[figure: A pentagonal based pyramid. The base is drawn as a pentagon standing on one side with its fifth vertex uppermost, and the apex above is joined to that vertex, to the two vertices either side of it, and to the lower-right vertex — four edges from the apex, the last of them crossing the face of the solid.]*

*[figure: A square base pyramid. The base is drawn as an upright rectangle and the apex above is joined to all four of its corners, so two edges run outside the rectangle and two cross it.]*

*[figure: A triangular base pyramid. The base triangle is drawn in perspective, its third vertex raised into the middle of the figure, and the apex above is joined to all three base vertices.]*

Every solid shape can be described by counting its faces (the flat surfaces), its edges (the lines where two faces meet), and its vertices (the corners). The table below counts these for the pyramids you have seen.

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triangular based Pyramid | 4 | 6 | 4 |
| Square based Pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |
| Pentagonal base Pyramid | 6 | 10 | 6 |
| Rectangular based Pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |

Notice the pattern: the number of faces, edges and vertices all grow together as the base gains more sides. A square based pyramid and a rectangular based pyramid each have the same counts — 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices — because each base has four sides. A pentagonal based pyramid, with a five-sided base, has one more of each: 6 faces, 10 edges and 6 vertices.

A cone is different from a pyramid, as you can see in fig-07. Its base is a flat circular face, and from it a single curved surface runs up to a pointed top. A cone has:

1. Two faces — one curved face and one flat face.
2. One edge — the circular line where the flat base meets the curved surface.
3. One vertex — the pointed top.

*[figure: Two drawings side by side. On the left an upright cone: an elliptical base with the two slant sides running up from the ends of the ellipse to the apex. On the right a cone lying on its side, apex at the left, its two slant sides reaching a narrow upright ellipse whose top and bottom then continue as two parallel lines to a second narrow ellipse further right.]*

Example. A hexagonal based pyramid has a base with six sides. How many faces, edges and vertices does it have?

Follow the pattern you saw in the table. The number of sides on the base gives the number of vertices: the base has six corners, and the apex adds one more, so the pyramid has 6 + 1 = 7 vertices. Its faces are the six triangular side faces plus the base itself, giving 6 + 1 = 7 faces. Each of the six base vertices joins the apex with one edge, and the base itself has six edges around it, so the pyramid has 6 + 6 = 12 edges.

So a hexagonal based pyramid has 7 faces, 12 edges and 7 vertices.

**Practice**

2. How many vertices has a tetrahedron?

3. A square based pyramid has how many faces, edges and vertices?
