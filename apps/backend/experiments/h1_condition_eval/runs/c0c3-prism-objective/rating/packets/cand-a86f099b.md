## The revision pack

### Properties of Cylinders and Spheres

Let's look closely at two three-dimensional shapes you meet all the time: the cylinder and the sphere. A cylinder is the shape of a tin or a drum — straight sides with a round top and a round base. A sphere is the shape of a ball or an orange — perfectly round.

Look at the cylinder in fig-01. It has a flat circular top and a flat circular base, and between them one face that curves all the way around the sides. So a cylinder has two circular faces and one curved face. Count them together and you find it has three faces altogether. Run your finger along the rim of the top circle: that is one curved edge, and the rim of the base circle is another. So it has two curved edges. Notice, too, that there is no sharp corner anywhere on a cylinder — no point where three or more edges meet. That means a cylinder has no vertex at all.

Now look at the sphere in fig-02. A sphere is a single curved surface, with nothing flat on it. There are no edges and no vertices anywhere on it. Finally, turn the sphere any way you like: it looks the same from every direction, so we say it is symmetrical from all directions.

*[figure: A sphere drawn as a circle with an ellipse across its middle to show the curved surface.]*

*[figure: A cylinder drawn upright: an ellipse for the top face, two vertical sides falling from its ends, and a curve closing the base.]*

Let's identify the properties of a cylinder together, one by one.

Look again at the cylinder in fig-01. Ask yourself four questions.

First, what faces does it have? You can see a flat circle at the top and a flat circle at the bottom, and the curved side running between them. That is two circular faces and one curved face — three faces in total.

Second, does it have any vertices? A vertex is a sharp corner where edges meet. Run your eye around the whole cylinder: there is no sharp corner anywhere. So it has no vertex.

Third, does it have any edges? An edge is a line where two faces meet. Where the curved face meets the top circle, there is a curved rim — that is one curved edge. Where it meets the base circle, there is another. So it has two curved edges.

Now do the same for the sphere in fig-02. Ask the same questions. It has only one face, and that face is curved — there is no flat circle anywhere. With no flat face meeting a curved one, there are no edges. And with no edges, there is no sharp corner, so there is no vertex either. Finally, spin the sphere: it looks the same from every direction, which is why we say it is symmetrical from all directions.

So you can list the properties of each shape in the same way: count the faces, count the edges, count the vertices, and note any special feature such as the cylinder's curved faces and edges, or the sphere's symmetry.

### Volume of a Cube, a Cuboid and a Prism

Volume tells us how much space a three-dimensional shape takes up, and it is measured in cubic units like cm³, m³. For a cuboid, think of it as a box that is made of layers of cubes stacked up. If you know the area of the base (how many cubes cover the floor of the box) and how many layers high it is, you can find the volume.

**Volume of a cuboid** = area of base × height = length × breadth × height, often written simply as **V = l × b × h** or **V = lbh**.

**Volume of a cube** is the same idea, but a cube has every edge the same length. So its volume is length × length × length, written as **V = l³**.

A prism is a solid whose two ends are identical. For any prism, you can again find the volume by multiplying the area of one end (the base) by the length it runs back: **Volume of a prism = area of base × height**. For a triangular prism, the base is a triangle, so you first find the area of that triangle using area = ½ × base × height of the triangle, then multiply by how far the prism runs.

**Example 1.** Calculate the volume of a cuboid of length 3 cm, breadth 2 cm and height 8 cm.

Volume of cuboid = l × b × h.

length = 3 cm, breadth = 2 cm, height = 8 cm.

Volume = 3 cm × 2 cm × 8 cm = 48 cm³.

**Example 2.** The volume of a cuboid is 24 cm³. Its base is 6 cm by 2 cm. Find its height.

Volume of cuboid = l × b × h.

length = 6 cm, breadth = 2 cm, height = h.

Volume = 24 cm³.

So 24 cm³ = 6 cm × 2 cm × h.

24 cm³ = 12 cm² × h.

Divide both sides by 12 cm²:

24 cm³ ÷ 12 cm² = h, so h = 2 cm.

**Example 3.** Calculate the volume of a cube of side 3 cm.

Volume of cube = l³.

V = (3 cm)³ = 3 cm × 3 cm × 3 cm = 27 cm³.

**Example 4.** fig-03 shows a triangular prism lying on its rectangular face. The triangular end has a base of 5 m and a height of 3 m, and the prism runs back 10 m. Find its volume.

First find the area of the triangular end (the base of the prism):

Area of triangle = ½ × base × height = ½ × 5 m × 3 m = 7.5 m².

Now multiply by the length the prism runs:

Volume of prism = area of base × height = 7.5 m² × 10 m = 75 m³.

   A sphere is one single curved surface with nothing flat on it. Since edges form where faces meet and vertices form where edges meet, a sphere has neither.

   Each rim is a line where the curved face meets a circular face. There is one such rim at the top and one at the base, so the cylinder has two curved edges.

1. 96 cm³

   V = l × b × h = 8 cm × 4 cm × 3 cm = 96 cm³.
