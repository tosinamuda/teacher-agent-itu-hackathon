## The revision pack

### What is Statistics, and Why Do We Need It?

Statistics is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data. The word <strong>data</strong> sits at the heart of it all, so let's be clear about what data means.

<strong>Data</strong> are facts or information collected for a particular purpose. The number of pupils in your class, the scores you got in your last test, or the amount of rain that fell each day last week are all examples of data. Each is a set of facts gathered for a reason — to answer a question or find something out. Wherever facts are gathered for a purpose, statistics is at work.

Why do we need statistics? Because it helps us to do five important things:

<strong>(a) Collect information</strong> — bring facts together in one place.
<strong>(b) Organize information</strong> — put the facts in order so they are easy to read and handle.
<strong>(c) Compare information</strong> — look at two or more sets of facts side by side.
<strong>(d) Make decisions</strong> — use the facts to choose what to do.
<strong>(e) Present information clearly</strong> — show the facts in a way that others can understand.

These five are the purpose of statistics: it turns scattered facts into useful, clear information that helps us decide and act.

<strong>Example.</strong> Your class wants to know how many pupils walk to school.

First you <strong>collect information</strong> by asking every pupil how they travel. Then you <strong>organize information</strong> by counting how many walk, how many ride a bicycle, and how many are dropped off by car. Now you can <strong>compare information</strong> to see which way most pupils travel, and <strong>present it clearly</strong> in a small table. Finally, you can <strong>make a decision</strong> — for instance, whether the school should ask for more bicycle racks.

Every step you just took — collecting, organizing, comparing, presenting and deciding — is statistics.

### Collecting, Tallying and Tabulating Data

When you want to find out something about a group, you first need to gather information about them. That gathered information is called <strong>data</strong>. There are four main ways to collect data:

1. <strong>Observation</strong> — you watch and note what you see, such as counting how many cars pass a spot.
2. <strong>Counting</strong> — you count items one by one.
3. <strong>Interview</strong> — you ask people questions and record their answers.
4. <strong>Questionnaire</strong> — you give people a written set of questions and collect their written answers.

Once you have collected data, you need a neat way to record it as it comes in. <strong>Tally marks</strong> are quick marks used to record data while you are collecting it. For every item you count, you draw one stroke. When you reach the fifth stroke, you draw it across the previous four to make a group like this: <strong>|||/|</strong> (four strokes with the fifth crossing them). Each group of five tally marks is easy to count later.

After tallying, you can set the data out in a <strong>frequency table</strong>. A frequency table is a table showing how many times each item occurs in a set of data. The column that lists each item's count is called the <strong>frequency</strong>.

<strong>Worked example — recording data with tally marks</strong>

Imagine you record the vehicles that pass by as: bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car.

To record this with tally marks, go through the list one item at a time. Start with the car column, adding a stroke for each car you meet. As you count the cars — car, car, car, car, car, car — you draw |||/| (five) then one more, giving six tally marks for car. Continue for the other vehicles.

Your tally table looks like this:

| Vehicles | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|
| car | |||/|| | 6 |
| bus | | | 1 |
| lorry | || | 2 |
| bicycle | ||| | 3 |
| motor bike | | | 0 |

Read the last column (the Total) as the count for each item: car occurs 6 times, bus 1 time, lorry 2 times and bicycle 3 times. Notice the motor bike appears in the table with a total of 0 because it did not occur in the data.

<strong>Worked example — a frequency table</strong>

A class is asked their favourite colour, and the counts are collected. The results are set out in a frequency table:

| Colour | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

The frequency column tells you how many students chose each colour. For instance, 30 students chose Pink and 14 chose Green. To check your table, you can add all the frequencies together to find the total number of students who answered.

So the whole process has three steps. First, <strong>collect</strong> the data using observation, counting, an interview or a questionnaire. Second, <strong>record</strong> the data with tally marks as it is collected. Third, <strong>tabulate</strong> — set the data out in a frequency table so you can read how many times each item occurs at a glance. Now try building a frequency table yourself.
