### Plane Shapes: Their Types, Properties, Similarities and Differences

A plane shape is a flat, closed shape drawn on a flat surface. We can name each plane shape by counting its sides (the straight or curved edges) and its corners (where two sides meet). The corners are also called vertices (one corner is a vertex).

Here are the plane shapes you will meet most often:

• A triangle has 3 sides and 3 vertices.
• A square has 4 equal sides and 4 right angles. All four sides are the same length.
• A rectangle has 4 sides and 4 right angles, but only the two opposite sides are equal.
• A parallelogram has 4 sides, with each pair of opposite sides parallel and equal. Its opposite angles are equal too.
• A trapezium has 4 sides, and only one pair of opposite sides is parallel.
• A circle is the special shape that has no straight sides and no vertices — it is one single curved edge that is always the same distance from its centre.

To compare shapes, look at their similarities and their differences.

Similarity: a square, a rectangle, a parallelogram and a trapezium are all quadrilaterals — each has 4 sides. So they are alike because they all have 4 sides and 4 vertices.

Difference: they differ in how many equal sides they have and how many right angles they have. A square has all 4 sides equal and all 4 angles right; a rectangle has opposite sides equal and all 4 angles right; a parallelogram has opposite sides equal but its angles are not all right angles (unless it is a rectangle); a trapezium has only one pair of parallel sides.

A square is the only shape in this group that is both a rectangle (all right angles) and a shape with all four sides equal.

Let's work through comparing two shapes together.

Suppose someone shows you a square and a parallelogram. Both have 4 sides, so both are quadrilaterals — that is one similarity. But now look closer: the square has all 4 sides equal and all 4 angles are right angles, while the parallelogram has only its opposite sides equal and, usually, no right angles at all. That difference lets you tell them apart.

Now think about a triangle and a trapezium. The triangle has 3 sides and 3 vertices; the trapezium has 4 sides and 4 vertices. Their difference is the number of sides. But they share a feature too: both can be divided into triangles when you draw a diagonal (a straight line joining two opposite vertices).

### The Parts of a Circle

A circle is a round plane shape. Every point on its edge is the same distance from a fixed point at its very centre. That fixed point is called the <strong>centre</strong> of the circle. Around the circle, there are several parts you should be able to name, and each one is a line or region with a special name.

Draw a circle and mark its centre with a dot. From the centre, draw a straight line to any point on the edge. That line is called a <strong>radius</strong>. A radius is the distance from the centre to the edge. You can draw many radii — every radius of the same circle is equal in length.

Now draw a straight line that passes through the centre and touches the edge at two opposite points. This line is called a <strong>diameter</strong>. The diameter is twice the radius — it is the longest straight line you can draw inside a circle.

Next, draw a straight line that joins two points on the edge but does <em>not</em> pass through the centre. This line is called a <strong>chord</strong>. Notice the difference: a diameter is a special chord that passes through the centre. Every diameter is a chord, but not every chord is a diameter.

The remaining parts are made from curves and regions of the circle.

The <strong>circumference</strong> is the whole distance all the way around the circle — the circle's perimeter. It is the curved boundary of the circle.

A curved part of the circumference between two points is called an <strong>arc</strong>. A short arc is a minor arc, and a long arc is a major arc.

When a chord cuts across the circle, it splits the circle into two regions. The smaller region between the chord and the arc is called a <strong>segment</strong>. (The larger region on the other side of the chord is the major segment.)

If you draw two radii to the edge, the slice of the circle between them — shaped like a slice of cake or a piece of pie — is called a <strong>sector</strong>.

Finally, a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point and never cuts through it is called a <strong>tangent</strong>. The tangent meets the circle at just one point, called the point of tangency, and the radius drawn to that point meets the tangent at a right angle.

<strong>Example.</strong> Draw a circle with its centre marked O. From O, draw a radius to a point A on the edge, and a diameter through O meeting the edge at A and B. Now draw a chord CD that does not pass through O. Shade the slice between the two radii OA and OB — that is a <strong>sector</strong>. Mark the curved part of the edge between C and D — that is an <strong>arc</strong>. The smaller region between chord CD and the arc is a <strong>segment</strong>. Draw a straight line that just touches the circle at point A — that is a <strong>tangent</strong>. The whole curved boundary of your circle is its <strong>circumference</strong>.

Check your drawing against these parts: centre (O), radius (OA), diameter (AB), chord (CD), arc, segment, sector, tangent, and circumference. You have now labelled every part of the circle.

### Finding the Perimeter of Plane Shapes

The **perimeter** of a plane shape is the total distance all the way around its outside edge. You find it by adding up the lengths of every side. For shapes with equal sides, there is often a quicker way, and for a circle the perimeter has its own name — the **circumference**.

Let's look at each shape you need to be able to measure around.

**Square** — all four sides are equal, so the perimeter is four times one side.

$$\text{Perimeter of a square} = 4 \times \text{side}$$

**Rectangle** — opposite sides are equal, so you add the length and breadth, then double.

$$\text{Perimeter of a rectangle} = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{breadth})$$

**Parallelogram** — opposite sides are equal, so it works just like the rectangle.

$$\text{Perimeter of a parallelogram} = 2 \times (\text{base} + \text{side})$$

**Trapezium** — has four sides of (usually) different lengths, so you simply add all four sides.

$$\text{Perimeter of a trapezium} = \text{side}_1 + \text{side}_2 + \text{side}_3 + \text{side}_4$$

**Regular polygon** — a regular polygon has all its sides equal, so multiply one side by the number of sides.

$$\text{Perimeter} = \text{number of sides} \times \text{length of one side}$$

A regular hexagon has 6 sides, a regular pentagon has 5 sides, and so on.

**Circle** — the perimeter of a circle is called its **circumference**. It is found using the radius (halfway across) or the diameter (all the way across, so the diameter is twice the radius).

$$\text{Circumference} = 2 \pi r \quad \text{or} \quad \pi d$$

where $r$ is the radius, $d$ is the diameter, and $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$ (or $3.14$).

**Example 1 — Square.** Find the perimeter of a square of side 8 cm.

All four sides are equal, so:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 4 \times 8 = 32 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 2 — Rectangle.** Find the perimeter of a rectangle of length 7 cm and breadth 4 cm.

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{breadth}) = 2 \times (7 + 4) = 2 \times 11 = 22 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 3 — Regular pentagon.** Find the perimeter of a regular pentagon with sides 5 cm.

A regular pentagon has 5 equal sides, so:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 5 \times 5 = 25 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 4 — Parallelogram.** Find the perimeter of a parallelogram whose base is 6 cm and side is 5 cm.

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2 \times (\text{base} + \text{side}) = 2 \times (6 + 5) = 2 \times 11 = 22 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 5 — Regular hexagon.** Find the perimeter of a regular hexagon with sides 5 cm.

A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides, so:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 6 \times 5 = 30 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 6 — Circle.** Find the circumference of a circle with radius 7 cm. Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

$$\text{Circumference} = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 2 \times 22 = 44 \text{ cm}$$

**Working backwards — finding the missing side.** Sometimes you are given the perimeter and must find a missing side or radius.

**Example 7.** The circumference of a circle is 22 cm. Find its radius. Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

$$\text{Circumference} = 2\pi r$$

$$22 = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r$$

Divide both sides by 2:

$$11 = \frac{22}{7} \times r$$

Multiply both sides by 7:

$$77 = 22r$$

Divide both sides by 22:

$$r = \frac{77}{22} = 3.5 \text{ cm}$$

**Example 8 — Rectangle with an unknown.** A rectangle is such that its length is twice its breadth. If the breadth is $d$, find the total length of its sides.

Length $= 2d$, breadth $= d$. The total length of the sides is the perimeter:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{breadth}) = 2 \times (2d + d) = 2 \times 3d = 6d$$
