## The revision pack

### Why We Use Statistics

Every day, you come across lots of facts and figures — how many goals a team scored, the temperature outside, the price of bread, your scores in a test. When these pieces of information are gathered together, we call them <strong>data</strong>. <strong>Statistics</strong> is the part of mathematics that deals with collecting data, organising it, and making sense of it.

Why do we need statistics? Its main purpose is to help us <strong>make decisions based on facts instead of guesswork</strong>. For example, a shopkeeper who records which snacks sell best each week can decide which ones to stock more of. A school that records how many students are absent each day can see when illnesses spread and take action. A football coach who notes how many goals each player scores can pick the strongest team.

Statistics does three main jobs:
<ul>
<li><strong>Collecting</strong> data — gathering facts from a source, such as a survey or a tally.</li>
<li><strong>Organising</strong> data — putting the facts in order so they are easy to read, often in a table.</li>
<li><strong>Interpreting</strong> data — reading the organised facts to answer questions and draw conclusions.</li>
</ul>

In this section you will learn the purpose of statistics and how to read the information that is laid out in a table.

<strong>Worked example 1 — reading a table</strong>

A class recorded how each student travelled to school one morning. The results were put into a table.

<table>
<tr><th>Way of travelling</th><th>Number of students</th></tr>
<tr><td>Walking</td><td>14</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bicycle</td><td>8</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bus</td><td>6</td></tr>
<tr><td>Car</td><td>4</td></tr>
</table>

<em>Question:</em> Which way of travelling was used by the most students?

<em>Answer:</em> Walking. The table shows 14 students walked, and no other row has a bigger number, so walking was the most common way.

The table lets us answer this question at a glance. If the same facts were just a long jumbled list of words, we would have to count through everything again. Organising the data in a table is what makes the information useful.

The purpose of statistics is not just to count things — it is to use the counts to <strong>decide and predict</strong>. When data is set out in a table, you can quickly find the biggest value, the smallest value, the total, and the difference between values. These simple readings let you answer real questions.

The steps you follow are:
<ol>
<li><strong>Read</strong> the headings of the table to know what each column and row mean.</li>
<li><strong>Compare</strong> the numbers to answer the question — look for the largest, the smallest, or the total.</li>
<li><strong>State</strong> your conclusion in a full sentence that uses the data you read.</li>
</ol>

Remember that a conclusion is only trustworthy when it comes from the data shown. If a number is not in the table, you cannot invent it.

<strong>Worked example 2 — comparing values in a table</strong>

A fruit seller recorded how many of each fruit he sold on Saturday.

<table>
<tr><th>Fruit</th><th>Fruits sold</th></tr>
<tr><td>Apples</td><td>25</td></tr>
<tr><td>Oranges</td><td>30</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bananas</td><td>18</td></tr>
<tr><td>Mangoes</td><td>12</td></tr>
</table>

<em>Question 1:</em> How many more oranges than mangoes were sold?

<em>Answer 1:</em> 30 − 12 = 18, so 18 more oranges were sold.

<em>Question 2:</em> How many fruits were sold altogether?

<em>Answer 2:</em> 25 + 30 + 18 + 12 = 85 fruits.

<em>Question 3:</em> Which fruit should the seller order more of next week?

<em>Answer 3:</em> Oranges, because they had the largest number sold (30). The table tells him which fruit people wanted most.

### Reading Stories from Tables of Data

When a survey or count is finished, the results are often long lists of numbers that are hard to take in at a glance. A table gathers those results into neat rows and columns so that we can see patterns, compare values, and answer questions quickly. Tables turn a messy pile of facts into a clear picture.

To read a table, look first at the <strong>heading</strong>, which tells you what was counted. Then read the <strong>row labels</strong> down the left and the <strong>column labels</strong> across the top. Where a row and a column meet, the number in that box is the answer to the question the two labels ask together.

Here is an example. The table below shows the favourite sport chosen by students in one school, with the number of students who chose each sport.

| Favourite sport | Number of students |
|---|---|
| Football | 40 |
| Basketball | 25 |
| Swimming | 20 |
| Athletics | 15 |
| <strong>Total</strong> | <strong>100</strong> |

Three questions you can answer from this table:

<ul>
<li>How many students chose swimming? Look along the "Swimming" row to the number column: <strong>20</strong>.</li>
<li>Which sport was most popular? The row with the biggest number is Football (<strong>40</strong>), so football was the most popular.</li>
<li>How many students took part altogether? The <strong>Total</strong> row at the bottom says <strong>100</strong>.</li>
</ul>

A "Total" row or column is a great help: it lets you check that all the parts add up, and it gives you a whole to compare each part against.

The word <strong>statistic</strong> is about collecting, organising and making sense of facts in numbers. Tables are one of the clearest ways to organise data. When you analyse a table, you look carefully at the numbers to spot what is <em>most</em> common, what is <em>least</em> common, and how the parts compare. When you interpret it, you explain in words what the numbers mean.

A good way to work through a table is to answer questions in this order:

<ol>
<li><strong>What does the table count?</strong> Read the heading and the labels.</li>
<li><strong>How much is each part?</strong> Read the number in each row or column.</li>
<li><strong>What is the total?</strong> Add the parts or read the total row.</li>
<li><strong>What is the pattern?</strong> Compare the parts to find the biggest, the smallest, and how they differ.</li>
</ol>

Following these steps turns a table of numbers into a sentence you can say aloud, like "Twice as many students walk to school as come by bicycle."

<strong>Worked example</strong>

The table below shows how students in a class travel to school each day.

| How students travel | Number of students |
|---|---|
| Walk | 30 |
| Bicycle | 15 |
| Bus | 10 |
| Car | 5 |
| <strong>Total</strong> | <strong>60</strong> |

Let's analyse and interpret it step by step.

<strong>Step 1 — What does the table count?</strong><br>
The heading tells us the number of students using each way of travelling to school.

<strong>Step 2 — How much is each part?</strong><br>
Walk: 30, Bicycle: 15, Bus: 10, Car: 5.

<strong>Step 3 — What is the total?</strong><br>
The Total row reads 60. Check: 30 + 15 + 10 + 5 = 60. ✓

<strong>Step 4 — What is the pattern?</strong><br>
Walking is the most common way (30), and travelling by car is the least common (5). Walking is used by twice as many students as bicycling (30 = 2 × 15).

<strong>Interpretation in words:</strong> Most students in the class walk to school, and very few come by car.
