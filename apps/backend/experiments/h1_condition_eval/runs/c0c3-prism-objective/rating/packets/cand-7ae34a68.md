### Types of Plane Shapes and Their Properties

Plane shapes are shapes that lie flat on a single surface, so they are also called two-dimensional shapes (2-D shapes). They have length and width but no thickness. Common plane shapes include the triangle, rectangle, square, parallelogram, trapezium, circle and other polygons.

Every plane shape can be described by its properties — the facts that are always true about it, such as how many sides it has, whether its sides are equal, whether its angles are equal, how many lines of symmetry it has, and how its diagonals behave. In this section you will learn to recognise each shape, state its properties, and tell how shapes are alike and how they differ.

Look at the three triangles in fig-01. Each tick drawn on a side is a mark that says "these sides are equal in length", and each arc drawn at a corner is a mark that says "these angles are equal".

• Isosceles triangle — the two slanted sides carry one tick each, so two adjacent sides are equal. Its base angles (the two angles at the bottom) are equal. It has one line of symmetry.

• Scalene triangle — the three sides carry one, two and three ticks, which means no two sides are equal. So no angle is equal to another. It has no line of symmetry.

• Equilateral triangle — all three sides carry one tick each, so all three sides are equal. That forces all three angles to be equal too, and each is 60°. It has three lines of symmetry.

These three triangles show how the same family of shapes can differ: the isosceles and equilateral both have a line of symmetry, the scalene has none; the equilateral has all sides equal, the isosceles has only two equal, and the scalene has none.

*[figure: Isosceles: a single tick on each of the two slanted sides, an arc at each base angle. Scalene: one, two and three ticks on the three sides, an arc at each vertex. Equilateral: a single tick on each of the three sides, an arc at each vertex.]*

Now compare the square and the rectangle in fig-02. Both are marked with a small right-angle square at each corner, which tells you every angle is a right angle (90°), and both have two pairs of parallel sides. Their difference is in the sides:

• Square — all four sides are equal, and all angles are 90°. It has four lines of symmetry. Its two diagonals cross (bisect) each other at a right angle.

• Rectangle — only the two opposite sides are equal (the longer sides match, the shorter sides match), and all angles are 90°. It has two lines of symmetry. Its diagonals bisect each other but not at a right angle.

So the square and the rectangle agree on their angles (all 90°) and their parallel sides, but they differ on whether the sides are all equal, how many lines of symmetry they have, and how their diagonals cross.

*[figure: Square: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the four sides. Rectangle: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the two longer sides and a double tick on each of the two shorter sides.]*

The parallelogram and the trapezium are both four-sided shapes, but they behave very differently. Look at fig-03: in each shape the single and double arrows on the sides show which pairs of sides are parallel.

• Parallelogram — it has two pairs of opposite sides that are equal and parallel. Opposite angles are equal. It has no line of symmetry. Its diagonals divide it into two equal triangles.

• Trapezium — only one pair of sides is parallel. Its opposite angles are not equal. It has no line of symmetry. Its diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles.

The parallelogram and trapezium share having no line of symmetry, but they differ because the parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides while the trapezium has only one.

*[figure: Parallelogram: a single arrow on each of the two horizontal sides, a double arrow on each of the two slanted sides. Trapezium: a single arrow on each of the two parallel sides, and one diagonal drawn in.]*

A polygon is a closed shape with three or more straight sides. Polygons are named by their number of sides. A handy pattern connects the number of sides to the number of triangles the polygon can be cut into when you draw diagonals from one vertex to every non-adjacent vertex: the number of triangles is the number of sides minus 2, that is, n − 2.

Here is the full list of the common polygons and the triangles each can be divided into:

Triangle 3 sides → 3 − 2 = 1 triangle
Quadrilateral 4 sides → 4 − 2 = 2 triangles
Pentagon 5 sides → 5 − 2 = 3 triangles
Hexagon 6 sides → 6 − 2 = 4 triangles
Heptagon 7 sides → 7 − 2 = 5 triangles
Octagon 8 sides → 8 − 2 = 6 triangles
Nonagon 9 sides → 9 − 2 = 7 triangles
Decagon 10 sides → 10 − 2 = 8 triangles
Hendecagon (undecagon) 11 sides → 11 − 2 = 9 triangles
Dodecagon 12 sides → 12 − 2 = 10 triangles

Worked example: How many triangles can an octagon be divided into? An octagon has 8 sides, so it divides into 8 − 2 = 6 triangles.

### The Parts of a Circle

A circle is the set of points that are all the same distance from one fixed point, called the centre. A circle has several named parts, and each one earns its name from where it lies or how it is drawn. Look at fig-04, which shows two circles with all the parts labelled, and read each one below.

- **Arc** — a curved part of the circumference. The first circle in fig-04 shows a small curved piece of the rim marked `arc`: an arc is simply part of the circle's curved edge.
- **Circumference** — the whole curved boundary of the circle. In fig-04 the arrow points to the curved rim of the first circle and labels it the circumference. The arc is part of this boundary.
- **Centre** — the fixed point inside the circle, the one all the points of the circle are the same distance from.
- **Radius** — any straight line joining the centre of the circle to the circumference. In the first circle of fig-04 a radius is drawn straight down from the centre to the rim. The plural of radius is radii.
- **Chord** — any straight line joining two points on the circumference of a circle. In the second circle of fig-04 a chord is drawn across the top, joining two points of the rim.
- **Diameter** — any chord that passes through the centre of the circle. In the first circle the straight line drawn across from one side of the rim to the other, through the centre, is the diameter. Every diameter is a chord, but only a chord that passes through the centre is a diameter.
- **Segment** — the region of a circle cut off by a chord. In the second circle of fig-04, the shaded region above the chord is a segment: it is the part of the circle bounded by a chord and the arc it cuts off.
- **Sector** — the region of a circle bounded by two radii and the arc between them. In the second circle the shaded wedge below the centre is a sector, with its two bounding lines both radii.
- **Tangent** — a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point. In the second circle the straight line drawn down the right-hand side, touching the rim at a single point, is a tangent.

*[figure: Two labelled circles. The first has the diameter drawn across it and a radius from its centre to the circumference, with the arc and the circumference labelled. The second has a chord near the top with the segment above it shaded, a shaded sector below with its right-hand bounding line labelled Radius, and a tangent touching it on the right.]*

Using the two circles in fig-04, check that you can point to each part and name it.

Take the first circle. The straight line drawn from the centre straight down to the rim is a **radius**, because it joins the centre to the circumference. The straight line drawn across the whole circle through the centre is a **diameter**, because it is a chord that passes through the centre. The curved rim all the way around is the **circumference**, and the small curved piece of it marked with an arrow is an **arc**.

Now take the second circle. The straight line across the top, joining two points of the rim, is a **chord**. The shaded region above that chord is a **segment**, because it is cut off from the circle by the chord. The shaded wedge below the centre, bounded by two radii and the arc between them, is a **sector**. The straight line on the right that just touches the circle at one point is a **tangent**.

### Perimeter of Plane Shapes

Perimeter is the distance all the way round a shape. To find it, you add the lengths of every side that makes up the outside of the shape.

Some shapes repeat the same length several times, so it helps to use a short formula:

- A **square** has four equal sides. If each side is $L$, the perimeter is $L+L+L+L = 4L$.
- A **rectangle** has two equal long sides (the length $L$) and two equal short sides (the breadth $b$). Its perimeter is $L+L+b+b = 2L + 2b = 2(L+b)$.
- A **trapezium** has four different sides, $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$, so its perimeter is simply $a+b+c+d$.
- A **parallelogram** has two pairs of equal sides. If the base is $a$ and the slanted side is $b$, its perimeter is $a+a+b+b = 2a+2b = 2(a+b)$.
- A **regular polygon** (like a regular pentagon or regular hexagon) has all its sides equal. If it has $n$ equal sides of length $s$, its perimeter is $n \times s$. A regular hexagon has 6 sides, so its perimeter is $6 \times s$.
- A **circle** has no straight sides. Its perimeter is called the **circumference**, and it is found with
  $$C = 2\pi r \quad \text{or} \quad C = \pi d$$
  where $r$ is the radius and $d = 2r$ is the diameter.

Sometimes you are told the perimeter and asked to find a missing side. Work backwards: write the formula, put in what you know, and solve for the unknown length.

**Example 1 — Square**
A square has sides of 15cm. Its perimeter is
$$L+L+L+L = 4L = 4(15) = 60\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 2 — Rectangle**
A rectangle has length 10cm and breadth 7cm. Its perimeter is
$$L+L+b+b = 2L + 2b = 2(10) + 2(7) = 20 + 14 = 34\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 3 — Finding a missing breadth**
The perimeter of a rectangle is 60cm and its length is 17cm. Find the breadth.
$$P = 2L + 2b$$
$$60 = 2(17) + 2b$$
$$60 = 34 + 2b$$
$$60 - 34 = 2b$$
$$2b = 26$$
$$b = \frac{26}{2} = 13\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 4 — Trapezium**
A trapezium has sides 11cm, 9cm, 5cm and 4cm. Its perimeter is
$$a+b+c+d = 11 + 9 + 5 + 4 = 29\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 5 — Parallelogram**
A parallelogram has base 9cm and slanted side 5cm. Its perimeter is
$$2a + 2b = 2(a+b) = 2(9+5) = 2(14) = 28\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 6 — Regular hexagon**
A regular hexagon has sides of 5.2cm. It has 6 equal sides, so
$$\text{perimeter} = 6 \times 5.2 = 31.2\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 7 — Circle**
The perimeter (circumference) of a circle of radius 7cm, using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$, is
$$C = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 2 \times 22 = 44\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 8 — Finding a missing radius**
The circumference of a circle is 440cm. Find the radius.
$$C = 2\pi r$$
$$440 = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r$$
$$440 = \frac{44r}{7}$$
Cross-multiplying,
$$440 \times 7 = 44r$$
$$3080 = 44r$$
$$r = \frac{3080}{44} = 70\mathrm{cm}.$$

**Example 9 — A shape with a curved part**
A shape is a quarter of a circle: two straight sides of 4.9cm each meet at a right angle, and an arc joins their ends. To find its perimeter, add the two straight sides to the length of the curved part.

The curved part is one quarter of the circumference of a full circle of radius 4.9cm:
$$\text{curved part} = \frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9$$
$$= \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{44}{7} \times 4.9 = \frac{11}{7} \times \frac{49}{10} = \frac{77}{10} = 7.7\mathrm{cm}.$$

Now add the two straight sides:
$$\text{perimeter} = 4.9 + 4.9 + 7.7 = 17.5\mathrm{cm}.$$
