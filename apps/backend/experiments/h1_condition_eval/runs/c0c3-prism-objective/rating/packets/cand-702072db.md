## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is For

You already know how to count things and record what you find — how many bottles are in the crate, how many pencils are in the tin. Statistics is the way we turn that kind of counting and recording into something useful.

Statistics is the branch of mathematics that deals with collecting, organising, and interpreting information. It is not just about counting for the sake of counting. The whole point is to take information that is scattered around — a list of names, a pile of counters, a row of marks — and arrange it so that it tells you something clear.

Think about it in three parts:

1. Collecting — you gather the information, like counting how many of your classmates are present.
2. Organising — you arrange that information in a neat way, such as putting the numbers in a table or a list.
3. Interpreting — you read the organised information to understand what it means, like seeing which day had the most pupils in school.

So the purpose of statistics is simple: it helps us collect, organise, and make sense of information. Whenever you have a lot of facts or numbers and you want to understand them, statistics is the tool you use to do it.

### Making Sense of a Frequency Table

Statistics is the branch of mathematics that helps us gather information, organise it, and make sense of it. When we want to answer a question like "Which colour do most students in my class like best?" or "How many children are in each family?", we first have to collect the data — that is, gather the facts or numbers. We can collect data by counting, by asking people (a questionnaire), by watching, or by recording what we observe.

Once the data is collected, it usually looks like a long, messy list. To understand it, we need to organise it. One of the best ways is to build a <strong>frequency table</strong>. A frequency table shows us each different value and how many times it appears. That "how many times" is called the <strong>frequency</strong>.</p>

<p>Here is the simple idea behind a frequency table: we take every piece of data, and for each value we make a mark. We often use <strong>tally marks</strong> — four vertical strokes, with the fifth drawn across them as a diagonal line, so we can count quickly in groups of five. After we have marked every piece of data, we count the marks for each value and write the total. That total is the frequency.</p>

<p>When the table is finished, we can <strong>interpret</strong> it — we can read the table to answer questions about the data, such as which value is the most common, which is the least common, or how many pieces of data there are altogether (the total of all the frequencies).</p>

<strong>Worked example</strong></p>

<p>A teacher asked each child in a class to say how many pets they have. Here are the answers from the class:</p>

<p>2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2, 0, 2, 1, 3, 2</p>

<p><strong>Step 1 — List the different values.</strong><br>
The answers we saw are 0, 1, 2 and 3. Write these in a column in order (smallest first): 0, 1, 2, 3.</p>

<p><strong>Step 2 — Tally the data.</strong><br>
Go through the list one number at a time and put a tally mark next to that value. For the first five numbers, "2, 1, 3, 2, 1":</p>

<ul>
<li>0: no mark yet</li>
<li>1: || (two marks — the 1s in positions 2 and 5)</li>
<li>2: || (two marks — the 2s in positions 1 and 4)</li>
<li>3: | (one mark — the 3 in position 3)</li>
</ul>

<p>Now continue through the rest of the list — "0, 2, 3, 1, 2, 0, 2, 1, 3, 2" — adding a mark each time. When you finish, count the marks for each value:</p>

<ul>
<li>0 appears in positions 6 and 11 → 2 marks</li>
<li>1 appears in positions 2, 5, 9, 13 → 4 marks</li>
<li>2 appears in positions 1, 4, 7, 10, 12, 15 → 6 marks</li>
<li>3 appears in positions 3, 8, 14 → 3 marks</li>
</ul>

<p>Write these as tally marks in a second column and the counts as a third column called <em>Frequency</em>:</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Number of pets</th><th>Tally</th><th>Frequency</th></tr>
<tr><td>0</td><td>||</td><td>2</td></tr>
<tr><td>1</td><td>||||</td><td>4</td></tr>
<tr><td>2</td><td>~~~|</td><td>6</td></tr>
<tr><td>3</td><td>|||</td><td>3</td></tr>
</table>

<p><strong>Check your tally.</strong><br>
The frequencies must add up to the total number of pieces of data. Here, 2 + 4 + 6 + 3 = 15, and there are exactly 15 numbers in the list — so the table is correct. If the frequencies did not add up to 15, we would know to re-tally.</p>

<p><strong>Interpreting the table.</strong><br>
Now we can read answers straight from the table:</p>

<ul>
<li>The most common number of pets is 2, because it has the highest frequency (6).</li>
<li>The least common number of pets is 0, because it has the lowest frequency (2).</li>
<li>The total number of children asked is 2 + 4 + 6 + 3 = 15.</li>
</ul>
