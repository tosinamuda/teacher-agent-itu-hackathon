### Constructing a Line Parallel to Another

Two straight lines are parallel when they never meet, however far you draw them, and stay the same distance apart all along their length. In this construction you are given a straight line AB and a point P, and your job is to draw a second line through P that is parallel to AB — a line that runs alongside AB, the same distance away at every point, never crossing it.

You will need a set square and a ruler. The set square has a right angle in one corner, and it is that corner (marked in the figures) that makes the construction work. Study fig-01: the line AB lies flat across the page, with the point P marked above it. Your first task is to line up the set square with AB, and the right angle is the part that keeps it true.

*[figure: Line $AB$, with a point $P$ marked above it. A set square is drawn below $AB$ with one edge along the line: the right angle is at the left end of that edge, one leg runs down from it, and the hypotenuse closes back to a point on $AB$ to the right.]*

<strong>Construct a line through P parallel to AB.</strong>

(a) Place the set square so that one of its straight edges lies accurately along the line AB, as in fig-01.

(b) Put the ruler against one of the other edges of the set square — the left-hand edge if you are right-handed. In fig-02 the ruler lies along the set square's downward edge; in fig-03 it rests against the sloping edge instead. Which edge you choose does not matter: the only thing that matters is that the ruler is held firmly against the set square and does not move once it is in place.

(c) Hold the ruler firmly and slide the set square along it towards P. Keep sliding until the edge that was lying along AB reaches P. Then draw a line along that edge of the set square, through P — this is shown in fig-04 and fig-05.

Why does this work? Sliding the set square along the held ruler moves it without turning it, so the edge that lay on AB keeps exactly the same direction the whole way. A line drawn through P along that edge therefore runs parallel to AB, the same distance away all along — which is precisely the line you wanted, as fig-05 shows.

*[figure: The same slide as fig-04, made against the leaning ruler of fig-03. The set square is drawn twice: once with its edge on $AB$, and once slid along the ruler until that edge passes through $P$. The two positions are congruent and point the same way — sliding along a held ruler moves the set square without turning it — so the edge through $P$ stays parallel to $AB$. The arrow gives the direction of the slide, along the ruler's edge, and the right angle is marked in both positions.]*

*[figure: The vertical ruler held fixed, with the set square drawn twice — once with its edge on $AB$ and once higher up, its edge on a second line parallel to $AB$ — and an arrow between the two positions pointing up along the ruler.]*

*[figure: The same set square on $AB$ as in fig-02, with the ruler moved to rest against its sloping edge instead of its upright one. The set square has not moved; only the ruler has, so it now leans across $AB$, running from lower left to upper right. The right angle is still where the edge on $AB$ meets the short upright edge, and it is marked there. The ruler's inclination does not affect the construction. The set square is drawn at one particular size, and with its right angle at one particular end; neither is part of the method. Any set square works, either way round, so long as one edge lies along $AB$ and the ruler rests against another.]*

*[figure: The same set square on $AB$, with the ruler now drawn as a long strip lying along the set square's downward edge. The right angle marked at the corner is the set square's own, between the edge lying along $AB$ and the edge running down from it.]*

**Practice**

1. You are given a line AB and a point P above it. What is the first step in constructing a line through P parallel to AB?

2. After the set square is lying on AB, what do you do with the ruler before you slide the set square?

3. While sliding the set square along the ruler towards P, what must you be careful to do, and why does that keep the line you draw parallel to AB?

### Constructing Perpendicular Lines

Two lines are said to be perpendicular to each other when they meet at a right angle — that is, at an angle of \(90^\circ\).

Look at fig-06. The line \(XY\) crosses the line \(AB\), with \(X\) above and \(Y\) below. The small square drawn at the crossing marks the right angle in the upper right quadrant. Because \(XY\) meets \(AB\) at \(90^\circ\), the two lines are perpendicular to each other.

*[figure: Line $AB$ with a second line $XY$ crossing it, $X$ above and $Y$ below, and a right-angle mark in the upper right quadrant at the crossing.]*

Let us construct a line perpendicular to a given line, using a set square and a ruler.

<strong>Step 1 — Draw the line and mark the point.</strong> Draw a line \(AB\) as in fig-07, and mark a point \(Q\) on the line where you want the perpendicular line to pass.

<strong>Step 2 — Place the set square.</strong> Place the edge of the set square on line \(AB\), so it sits on the line as shown in fig-08. Now hold the ruler so that its edge meets the hypotenuse (the long sloping edge) of the set square, as the figure shows.

<strong>Step 3 — Slide and draw.</strong> Pressing firmly on the ruler so it does not move, slide the set square down along the hypotenuse until the third edge of the set square touches point \(Q\). Then draw a line along that edge, from \(X\) above the line down to \(Y\) below it. Fig-09 shows the result: the line \(XY\) now crosses \(AB\) at a right angle, marked with the small square.

Because the third edge of the set square is perpendicular to its base, the line you drew is perpendicular to \(AB\). The two lines meet at \(90^\circ\), so \(XY \perp AB\).

*[figure: Line $AB$ with the set square standing on it as a narrow right-angled triangle: a short base along $AB$, the upright edge rising from the right end of that base with the right angle marked there, and the hypotenuse closing back down to the left.]*

*[figure: A plain line with a stroke at each end, labelled $A$ on the left and $B$ on the right.]*

The key point to remember: a line drawn perpendicular to another line meets it at a right angle, which is \(90^\circ\). The set square has a built-in right angle, so sliding it along the ruler and drawing along its third edge gives you a line that is exactly perpendicular to your line.

*[figure: Line $AB$ with $XY$ now drawn across it, $X$ above and $Y$ below, and a right-angle mark in the upper right quadrant.]*

**Practice**

1. When are two lines said to be perpendicular to each other?

2. Name the two instruments you use to construct a line perpendicular to a given line.

3. In the construction of a perpendicular line, what do you press firmly on so that it does not move while you slide the set square?

4. A line \(PQ\) is drawn so that it crosses line \(AB\) and makes an angle of \(90^\circ\) with it. What is the relationship between \(PQ\) and \(AB\)?

### Bisecting a line segment

To <strong>bisect</strong> a line segment means to divide it into two equal halves. The point where the segment is cut into those two equal halves is called its <strong>midpoint</strong>. Using a pair of compasses and a ruler, you can find that midpoint exactly — and, as a bonus, the line you draw to do it meets the segment at a right angle (90°).

You need a mathematical set for this. Here is the whole procedure.

<strong>Step 1.</strong> Draw the line segment you want to bisect and label its ends, say $A$ on the left and $B$ on the right (fig-10).

<strong>Step 2.</strong> Open your pair of compasses so that the radius (the gap between the sharp point and the pencil) is about $\frac{3}{4}$ of the length of $AB$.

<strong>Step 3.</strong> Place the sharp point of the compasses on $A$. Draw two arcs: one above the middle of $AB$ and one below the middle of $AB$.

<strong>Step 4.</strong> Keeping the same radius (do not change the compass opening), place the sharp point of the compasses on $B$. Draw two more arcs so that they cut the first pair of arcs — one crossing above $AB$ and one crossing below. Label the upper crossing $P$ and the lower crossing $Q$ (fig-11). Each small X is a point where an arc from $A$ meets an arc from $B$.

<strong>Step 5.</strong> Join $P$ to $Q$ with your ruler (fig-12).

<strong>Step 6.</strong> The line $PQ$ crosses $AB$ at $M$. $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, so $AM = MB$, and $PQ$ meets $AB$ at $90^\circ$.

*[figure: The same arcs with $P$ joined to $Q$, the joining line crossing $AB$ at its middle at $M$ and carrying a right-angle mark in the upper right quadrant.]*

*[figure: The segment $AB$ with arc pairs crossing above and below it, the upper crossing labelled $P$ and the lower one $Q$. Each crossing is drawn as a small X where an arc from $A$ meets an arc from $B$.]*

*[figure: The line segment $AB$, a stroke at each end, $A$ on the left and $B$ on the right.]*

<strong>Worked example — bisecting the segment $XY$</strong>

Suppose you have a segment $XY$ and you want to bisect it.

1. Open your compasses so the radius is about $\frac{3}{4}$ of $XY$.
2. Put the sharp point on $X$. Draw one arc above the middle of $XY$ and one below it.
3. Without changing the compass opening, put the sharp point on $Y$. Draw two more arcs so they cut the first pair — one crossing above and one crossing below.
4. The upper crossing is $P$ and the lower crossing is $Q$.
5. Join $P$ to $Q$ with a ruler. This line crosses $XY$ at its midpoint $M$.
6. Read off the result: $XM = MY$, and $PQ$ meets $XY$ at $90^\circ$.

**Practice**

1. What does it mean to bisect a line segment $AB$? Where is the point where the bisecting line crosses $AB$?

2. You draw the two arcs from $A$ with a certain compass opening. When you move the sharp point to $B$, what must you do with the compasses before drawing the second pair of arcs, and why?

3. After you join $P$ to $Q$ with a ruler, the line $PQ$ crosses $AB$ at $M$. What two facts do you know about $M$ and about how $PQ$ meets $AB$?

4. A segment $PQ$ is 12 cm long. If you bisect it, how long is each half, and what angle does the bisecting line make with $PQ$?

### Constructing a 90° Angle

You can build a $90^\circ$ angle with nothing but your ruler and compasses. The idea is to raise a perpendicular from a point on a straight line. Here is how it works, one step at a time.

1. Draw a straight line and mark a point $C$ on it — that is the point where the $90^\circ$ angle is to be built. In the figure (fig-13) the line runs from $A$ on the left to $B$ on the right, and $C$ sits near the middle.
2. Put the point of your compasses at $C$ and draw an arc that cuts the line at two points. Call those two points $D$ and $E$, one on each side of $C$.
3. Now open your compasses WIDER than the width $DC$ you used in step 2. This wider setting is essential: if you kept the old width, arcs drawn from $D$ and $E$ would only meet back at $C$ itself, and that gives you nothing new. With the compasses at $D$ at this new wider setting, draw an arc above the line.
4. Keeping the compasses at the same wider setting, place the point at $E$ and draw another arc above the line, cutting the first arc at a point $F$.
5. Join $C$ to $F$ with your ruler.
6. The line $CF$ is perpendicular to $AB$, so the angle between $CF$ and the line, $\angle FCB$, is exactly $90^\circ$.

*[figure: A line with a stroke at each end, $A$ on the left and $B$ on the right, and four short strokes standing on the line, a pair towards $A$ and a pair towards $B$. A perpendicular rises from a point near the middle, with a right-angle mark at its foot, and two short strokes cross each other near its top.]*

Let's watch the whole construction come together. In fig-13 you can see a line $AB$ with a point $C$ marked on it. Starting from $C$, an arc is drawn that cuts the line in two places — the four short strokes standing on the line show those two cuts, a pair towards $A$ and a pair towards $B$; together they are the two points $D$ and $E$.

Then the compasses are opened wider than the first width, and arcs are struck from $D$ and $E$ above the line. The two short strokes crossing each other near the top of the perpendicular show where those wider arcs meet — that is point $F$.

Finally a ruler joins $C$ to $F$. The perpendicular rises from $C$, and the little square at its foot marks the right angle: $\angle FCB = 90^\circ$.

The key move to remember is step 3 — you must open the compasses wider than $DC$ before drawing the arcs from $D$ and $E$. With the old width the two arcs would only meet back at $C$, so you would never reach $F$.

**Practice**

1. Write down the steps, in order, you would take to construct a $90^\circ$ angle at a point $C$ on a straight line $AB$.

2. In constructing a $90^\circ$ angle at point $C$ on a line, you first draw an arc with compasses at $C$ to mark points $D$ and $E$. Explain why you must open the compasses WIDER than $DC$ before drawing the next arcs from $D$ and $E$.

3. You have carried out the construction and joined $C$ to $F$, where $F$ is where the two wider arcs met. What can you say about the angle between $CF$ and the original line $AB$? What is the size of $\angle FCB$?

### Constructing a 60° Angle

An angle of 60° is one you can build with just a ruler and a pair of compasses, using two arcs of the same radius. The key idea: when you swing an arc of a fixed radius from a point, every point on that arc is the same distance from the centre. Two arcs of the *same* radius from two different centres will meet at a point that is that same distance from both — and that equal spacing is what makes the angle 60°.

The construction needs just three moves. You can follow them on fig-14.

*[figure: The line $BC$ with $B$ at the left, a stroke marking $X$ part way along, a long arc swung from $B$ through $X$ and up to the left, a shorter arc swung from $X$ cutting it, and a straight line from $B$ to that crossing.]*

**Constructing a 60° angle at B, along the line BC**

1. **Draw the line.** Draw a line segment and mark a point B on it where the angle will sit. Mark the far end of the line C. Set your compasses to any convenient radius — it can be any size that fits comfortably on your page.

2. **First arc from B.** With the compass point on B, draw an arc that cuts the line BC at a point. Call that point X. (On fig-14 this is the long arc swung from B through X.)

3. **Second arc from X.** Keep the compasses at exactly the *same* radius — do not change the setting. Now put the compass point on X and draw another arc that cuts the first arc. Call the point where the two arcs cross A. (On fig-14 this is the shorter arc swung from X cutting the first.)

4. **Join A to B.** Draw a straight line from B to A.

The angle between line BC and line BA is exactly 60°. Because both arcs were drawn with the same radius, the distance from B to A equals the distance from B to X, which equals the distance from X to A — so the triangle BAX has all three sides equal, and a triangle with three equal sides has all three angles 60°.

**Practice**

1. Construct a 60° angle at the left-hand end of a horizontal line, following the steps above. Label the line, the first arc's cutting point, and the crossing point of the two arcs, then join up to complete the angle.

**Constructing a 90° Angle**

1. a. Mark point $C$ on the line $AB$.
   b. With compasses at $C$, draw an arc cutting the line at two points $D$ and $E$.
   c. Open the compasses wider than $DC$, and with the point at $D$ draw an arc above the line.
   d. With the same wider setting at $E$, draw another arc cutting the first at $F$.
   e. Join $C$ to $F$ with a ruler.
   f. $CF$ is perpendicular to $AB$, so $\angle FCB = 90^\circ$.

   Follow the six steps of the construction exactly, ending with the perpendicular $CF$.

2. With the original width $DC$, the arcs drawn from $D$ and $E$ would only meet again back at $C$ itself, so you would not reach the new point $F$. Opening wider lets the two arcs meet at a point $F$ above the line, which is what joins to $C$ to make the perpendicular.

   The step-2 width is exactly the distance from $C$ to each of $D$ and $E$, so arcs of that width centred at $D$ and $E$ both pass through $C$ and meet only there. A wider opening makes them cross at a fresh point $F$ above the line.

3. $CF$ is perpendicular to $AB$, so $\angle FCB = 90^\circ$.

   Since $CF$ was drawn perpendicular to $AB$, the angle at the foot of the perpendicular is a right angle, $90^\circ$.
