## The revision pack

### Cylinders and Spheres: Shapes That Are Curved

Two three-dimensional shapes you meet all the time are the cylinder and the sphere. A drinks tin is a cylinder; a ball is a sphere. Even though they look quite different from the cubes and cuboids you already know, you can describe each one by its faces, edges and vertices.

Look at the cylinder in fig-01. A cylinder stands on a circular base and has a circular top. That means it has <strong>two circular faces</strong> — one on the top and one on the bottom. Around the outside, between those two circles, runs a single smooth surface that curves all the way round: the <strong>curved face</strong>. So a solid cylinder has <strong>three faces altogether</strong> — two circular and one curved.

Now count edges and vertices. The curved face meets the top circle along one edge and the bottom circle along another, so a cylinder has <strong>two curved edges</strong>. But where do the faces come together to make a point? They never do — the curved face simply wraps around. So a cylinder has <strong>no vertex</strong>.

*[figure: A cylinder drawn upright: an ellipse for the top face, two vertical sides falling from its ends, and a curve closing the base.]*

Now turn to the sphere in fig-02. A sphere is the shape of a ball — every point on its surface is the same distance from its centre. Because of that, a sphere looks identical from every direction: it is <strong>symmetrical from all directions</strong>.

Unlike the cylinder, a sphere has no flat face at all. Its whole surface is one single <strong>curved surface</strong>. And because there is nothing flat and no line where faces meet, a sphere has <strong>no edge and no vertex</strong>.

*[figure: A sphere drawn as a circle with an ellipse across its middle to show the curved surface.]*

### Volume of a Cube, a Cuboid and a Prism

Volume is the amount of space a three-dimensional shape fills, and we measure it in cubic units such as cm³ or m³. For a cuboid, think of the base as being made of layers that are stacked up to the height, so the volume is found by multiplying its three dimensions together:

Volume of cuboid = length × breadth × height = l × b × h

A cube is a special cuboid whose three sides are all the same length, so we simply multiply the side by itself three times:

Volume of cube = length × length × length = l³

Calculate the volume of a cuboid of length 3cm, breadth 2cm and height 8cm.

Volume of cuboid = l × b × h
= 3cm × 2cm × 8cm
= 48cm³

Sometimes you know the volume and the base, and you need to find the height. The volume of a cuboid is 24cm³ and its base is 6cm by 2cm. Find its height.

Volume = l × b × h
24cm³ = 6cm × 2cm × h
24cm³ = 12cm² × h

Divide both sides by 12cm²:
24cm³ ÷ 12cm² = h
2cm = h

So the height of the cuboid is 2cm.

Calculate the volume of a cube of side 3cm.

Volume of cube = l³
= 3cm × 3cm × 3cm
= 27cm³

A prism has a uniform cross-section, so its volume is found by multiplying the area of its base by the height (the distance it runs back). Look at the triangular prism in fig-03. Its end is a triangle with base 5m and height 3m, and the prism runs back 10m.

First find the area of the triangular face:
Area of triangle = ½ × b × h = ½ × 5m × 3m = 7.5m²

Then find the volume:
Volume of prism = area of base × height
= 7.5m² × 10m
= 75m³

*[figure: A triangular prism lying on its rectangular face. The triangular end has a base of 5 m and a height of 3 m, and the prism runs back 10 m.]*

3. What is the volume of a cube of side 4cm?

   Volume = l³ = 4cm × 4cm × 4cm = 64cm³.
