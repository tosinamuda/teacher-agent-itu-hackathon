## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is For

When you count how many of your friends have dogs, or how many days it rained last month, you are gathering information. That information is called <strong>data</strong>. Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose.

<strong>Statistics</strong> is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data. It is the whole process that turns a pile of scattered facts into something useful. Instead of just gathering numbers and leaving them in a messy list, statistics helps you work with them so you can actually understand what they mean.

Statistics matters because it helps us to:

- <strong>collect</strong> information — gather the facts we need;
- <strong>organise</strong> information — sort and arrange the facts so they are easier to read;
- <strong>compare</strong> information — put facts side by side to see how they differ;
- <strong>make decisions</strong> — choose what to do based on the facts;
- <strong>present</strong> information clearly — show the facts so others can understand them.

For example, a school records each learner's name in a register. That is collecting data. If you count how many learners are in each class and write the totals in a table, you are organising and presenting the data clearly. The table lets you compare classes and decide, say, which class has the most learners.

Let's see statistics at work with a small example.

Aminu asks five friends how many brothers each of them has. The answers are: 2, 1, 3, 1, 2.

- <strong>Collecting</strong>: Aminu asked the friends and wrote down their answers — the facts were gathered.
- <strong>Organising</strong>: He sorts them into a table, listing each number and how many friends gave it. So "1" appears twice, "2" appears twice, and "3" appears once.
- <strong>Presenting clearly</strong>: He now sees the organised table at a glance, so anyone reading it understands the answers without re-asking.
- <strong>Comparing and deciding</strong>: He can compare the answers and see that more friends have 1 or 2 brothers than have 3. If he needs to guess a typical answer, he has the facts to decide on.

### From Collected Data to a Frequency Table

Before you can make sense of a set of facts, you need to gather them first. In statistics, the facts we collect are called **data**, and data can be collected in four main ways:

1. **Observation** — watching and noting what happens.
2. **Counting** — counting objects one by one.
3. **Interview** — asking people questions and writing down their answers.
4. **Questionnaire** — giving people a written list of questions to fill in.

For example, if you want to know which vehicles pass your school gate in one morning, you might watch and count each one as it goes by. That is collecting data.

Once you have collected your data, you need to record it clearly as it comes in. A handy way to do this is with **tally marks**. A tally is a small stroke you make every time an item appears. You make four upright strokes, and the fifth stroke crosses the four to make a bundle of five, like this: <span>||||</span>. This makes the final count easy to read.

A **frequency table** is a table that shows how many times each item occurs in a set of data. Each different item is listed once, and next to it you write its **frequency** — the number of times that item appears. Let's see how a set of data becomes a frequency table.

**Example — collecting data with tally marks**

One morning you note the vehicles that pass your gate in this order:

bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car

Go through the list from the start. Each time you see a vehicle, add one tally mark beside its name. When you reach five marks for one vehicle, bundle them as a crossed group of five, and start a new group.

Car appears at positions 2, 3, 4, 8, 9 and 12 — six times, so it gets five marks bundled plus one extra: <span>||||</span> |
Bus appears once: <span>|</span>
Lorry appears at positions 5 and 10 — twice: <span>||</span>
Bicycle appears at positions 6, 7 and 11 — three times: <span>|||</span>

Now count each group of tally marks and write the result in the **Total** column. This gives the tally table:

| Vehicles | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|
| car | <span>||||</span> | | 6 |
| bus | | | 1 |
| lorry | || | 2 |
| bicycle | ||| | 3 |
| Motor Bike | | | 0 |

The **Total** column is the frequency of each vehicle — how many times it appeared in your data. You can check your work by adding the totals: 6 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 12, which matches the twelve vehicles you collected.

**Example — a frequency table**

When you already have the collected data and want to know how often each item occurs, you set it out in a frequency table. Suppose a class of students is asked their favourite colour, and their answers are counted. The frequency table records each colour once and shows how many students chose it:

| Colour | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

The frequency of Blue is 20, which means 20 students chose blue as their favourite colour. The frequency of Pink is 30, the highest, so pink was the most popular. Reading a frequency table like this lets you analyse and interpret your data at a glance.
