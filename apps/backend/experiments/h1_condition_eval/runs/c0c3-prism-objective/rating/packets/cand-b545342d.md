### Properties of Cubes and Cuboids

Three dimensional shapes are shapes that have length, breadth and height. Because they take up space in all three directions, they are also called solid shapes.

Solid shapes may be regular or irregular. Regular shapes are those with a regular, familiar form — a cube of sugar, a drink can, a match box. Irregular shapes are shapes without that regular form, like a stone or a tree.

Before we list the properties of a cube and a cuboid, here are three words you need. An <strong>edge</strong> is a line where two faces meet; it may be straight or curved. A <strong>face</strong> (or surface) may be flat or curved. A <strong>vertex</strong> (plural: vertices) is a point corner where edges meet. Look at fig-02: the leader line to the flat part is the <em>face</em>, the point corner is the <em>vertex</em>, and the line where two faces meet is the <em>edge</em>.

*[figure: A cuboid drawn the same way, wide and shallow, with three leader lines to the right naming the top face, the nearer top-right corner and the nearer right-hand vertical edge.]*

A <strong>cube</strong> is drawn in fig-01. Look at it and count its parts:

1. It is a solid shape.
2. It has <strong>6 square faces</strong>.
3. It has <strong>12 edges</strong>.
4. It has <strong>8 vertices</strong>.

A <strong>cuboid</strong> is drawn in fig-02. Compare it with the cube:

1. It has <strong>6 faces</strong>.
2. It has <strong>12 edges</strong>.
3. It has <strong>8 vertices</strong>.

The cube and the cuboid are the same in how many faces, edges and vertices they have. What makes them different is the shape of the faces: every face of a cube is a square, while the faces of a cuboid can be rectangles.

*[figure: A cube drawn as a see-through solid: two equal squares, the nearer one offset down and to the right of the farther one, with the four pairs of corresponding corners joined.]*

### Pyramids and Cones: Naming Them and Counting Their Parts

A pyramid is named after the shape of its base. A triangular base gives a triangular based pyramid, a square base gives a square based pyramid, a pentagonal base gives a pentagonal based pyramid, and a rectangular base gives a rectangular based pyramid. The apex is the single point above the base, and every other corner of the base is joined up to that apex.

Every pyramid is built from the same three parts:
<ul>
  <li><strong>Faces</strong> — the flat surfaces. A pyramid has its base plus one triangular face for each side of the base.</li>
  <li><strong>Edges</strong> — the lines where two faces meet.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertices</strong> — the corners where edges meet.</li>
</ul>

The table below shows the counts for the four pyramids you can see in the figures.

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triangular based pyramid (tetrahedron) | 4 | 6 | 4 |
| Square based pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |
| Pentagonal based pyramid | 6 | 10 | 6 |
| Rectangular based pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |

Notice the pattern: each time the base gains one more side, the faces, edges and vertices each go up by one.

The cone is a different shape from a pyramid. Its parts are:
<ul>
  <li><strong>Two faces</strong> — one curved face (the sloping side) and one flat face (the base).</li>
  <li><strong>One edge</strong> — the single circular line where the curved face meets the flat base.</li>
  <li><strong>One vertex</strong> — the apex, the point at the top.</li>
</ul>
So a cone has 2 faces, 1 edge and 1 vertex. You can see the upright cone and the cone lying on its side in fig-07.

*[figure: Two drawings side by side. On the left an upright cone: an elliptical base with the two slant sides running up from the ends of the ellipse to the apex. On the right a cone lying on its side, apex at the left, its two slant sides reaching a narrow upright ellipse whose top and bottom then continue as two parallel lines to a second narrow ellipse further right.]*

Let's name and count the parts of a square based pyramid.

<strong>Name it.</strong> The base is a square, so the shape is called a <em>square based pyramid</em>.

<strong>Count the faces.</strong> There is the square base, and one triangular face for each of the four sides of that base. So it has 1 base + 4 triangular faces = 5 faces.

<strong>Count the edges.</strong> The base has 4 edges, and each of the 4 triangular faces reaches up to the apex, adding 4 slanting edges. So it has 4 + 4 = 8 edges.

<strong>Count the vertices.</strong> There are 4 corners on the base plus the apex = 5 vertices.

So a square based pyramid has 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices — exactly what the table says.

*[figure: A triangular base pyramid. The base triangle is drawn in perspective, its third vertex raised into the middle of the figure, and the apex above is joined to all three base vertices.]*

*[figure: A square base pyramid. The base is drawn as an upright rectangle and the apex above is joined to all four of its corners, so two edges run outside the rectangle and two cross it.]*

*[figure: A pentagonal based pyramid. The base is drawn as a pentagon standing on one side with its fifth vertex uppermost, and the apex above is joined to that vertex, to the two vertices either side of it, and to the lower-right vertex — four edges from the apex, the last of them crossing the face of the solid.]*

*[figure: A rectangular based pyramid, drawn like the square one but on a base wider than it is tall.]*
