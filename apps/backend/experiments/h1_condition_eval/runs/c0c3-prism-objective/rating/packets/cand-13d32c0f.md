### Naming Plane Shapes and Their Properties

Plane shapes are also known as two-dimensional (2-D) shapes. They lie flat on a surface, and the shapes you will meet are the triangle, the rectangle, the square, the parallelogram, the trapezium, the circle and other polygons.

A polygon is a closed shape that has three or more straight sides. Polygons are named by the number of sides they have. The table below lists the common ones, together with the number of triangles you can divide each polygon into by drawing diagonals from one vertex to all the non-adjacent vertices.

| Name of polygon | Number of sides | Number of triangles |
|---|---|---|
| Triangle | 3 | 1 |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 2 |
| Pentagon | 5 | 3 |
| Hexagon | 6 | 4 |
| Heptagon | 7 | 5 |
| Octagon | 8 | 6 |
| Nonagon | 9 | 7 |
| Decagon | 10 | 8 |
| Hendecagon (or undecagon) | 11 | 9 |
| Dodecagon | 12 | 10 |

Notice the pattern: the number of triangles is always two fewer than the number of sides, because you subtract 2 from the number of sides. So an octagon, with 8 sides, divides into 8 − 2 = 6 triangles; a pentagon, with 5 sides, divides into 5 − 2 = 3 triangles.

Now look at the three kinds of triangles in fig-01. The tick marks on the sides show which sides are equal (a single tick means "equal to the other single tick", and one, two or three ticks show three different lengths), and the arcs at the corners mark the angles.

An <strong>isosceles</strong> triangle has two adjacent sides that are equal, its two base angles are equal, and it has one line of symmetry.

A <strong>scalene</strong> triangle has no side equal to another, no angle equal to another, and no line of symmetry.

An <strong>equilateral</strong> triangle has all three sides equal, all three angles equal (each measuring 60°), and three lines of symmetry.

Here is how they compare:

| Isosceles | Scalene | Equilateral |
|---|---|---|
| 2 adjacent sides are equal | No side is equal | 3 sides are equal |
| Base angles are equal | No angle is equal to another | All angles are equal (60° each) |
| One line of symmetry | No line of symmetry | Three lines of symmetry |

The main difference between them is how many sides are equal: an equilateral has all three equal, an isosceles has just two equal, and a scalene has none equal. That is why the equilateral has three lines of symmetry, the isosceles has one, and the scalene has none.

*[figure: Isosceles: a single tick on each of the two slanted sides, an arc at each base angle. Scalene: one, two and three ticks on the three sides, an arc at each vertex. Equilateral: a single tick on each of the three sides, an arc at each vertex.]*

fig-02 shows a square and a rectangle side by side. In both, a small square mark at each corner marks a right angle (90°), and the tick marks on the sides show which sides are equal.

A <strong>square</strong> has all four sides equal, two pairs of parallel sides, all four angles equal (each 90°), four lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other at a right angle.

A <strong>rectangle</strong> has two opposite sides equal (the two longer sides match, and the two shorter sides match), two pairs of parallel sides, all four angles equal (each 90°), two lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other but not at a right angle.

| Square | Rectangle |
|---|---|
| All sides are equal | 2 opposite sides are equal |
| 2 pairs of parallel sides | 2 pairs of parallel sides |
| All angles are equal (90° each) | All angles are equal (90° each) |
| Four lines of symmetry | Two lines of symmetry |
| Diagonals bisect it at a right angle | Diagonals bisect it but not at a right angle |

The two shapes agree on parallel sides and right angles. They differ on the sides (a square has all four equal, a rectangle only opposite ones equal), on the number of lines of symmetry (4 for a square, 2 for a rectangle), and on whether the diagonals cut at a right angle.

*[figure: Square: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the four sides. Rectangle: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the two longer sides and a double tick on each of the two shorter sides.]*

fig-03 shows a parallelogram and a trapezium. The arrows on the sides mark the sides that are parallel (a single arrow on two sides means those two sides are parallel to each other, and a double arrow marks the other pair).

A <strong>parallelogram</strong> has two pairs of opposite sides that are equal and parallel, its opposite angles are equal, it has no line of symmetry, and its diagonals divide it into two equal triangles.

A <strong>trapezium</strong> has only one pair of parallel sides, its opposite angles are not equal, it has no line of symmetry, and its diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles.

| Parallelogram | Trapezium |
|---|---|
| 2 pairs of opposite sides are equal and parallel | Only a pair of sides are parallel |
| Opposite angles are equal | Opposite angles are not equal |
| No line of symmetry | No line of symmetry |
| Diagonals divide it into equal triangles | Diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles |

The key difference is the number of parallel sides: the parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides, while the trapezium has only one pair. Because of that, the parallelogram's opposite angles are equal and its diagonals make equal triangles, while the trapezium's are not equal and do not.

*[figure: Parallelogram: a single arrow on each of the two horizontal sides, a double arrow on each of the two slanted sides. Trapezium: a single arrow on each of the two parallel sides, and one diagonal drawn in.]*

### The Parts of a Circle

A circle is a plane shape drawn so that every point on its edge is the same distance from one fixed point inside it, called its **centre**. Like every plane shape, a circle has parts, and being able to name them is the first step to working with circles. Look at fig-04, where two circles are drawn with their parts labelled.

The **circumference** is the whole curved line that forms the circle itself — its outer boundary. Imagine running a pencil all the way around the circle without lifting it; the line you trace is the circumference.

A small curved part of the circumference is called an **arc**. If you draw a circle and only trace a portion of its curved edge, that portion is an arc. The first circle in fig-04 shows an arc marked along part of its circumference.

The **radius** is any straight line that joins the centre of the circle to the circumference. It is the distance from the centre out to the edge. In fig-04, the first circle has a radius drawn from its centre down to the circumference, and the second circle shows one of its sector's edges labelled Radius too. Every circle has many radii, and they are all the same length — that is what "every point on the edge is the same distance from the centre" means.

The **chord** is any straight line joining two points on the circumference. It runs across the inside of the circle from one point on the edge to another. The second circle in fig-04 shows a chord drawn near the top.

The **diameter** is a special chord: any chord that passes through the centre of the circle. Because it goes through the centre, the diameter is the longest straight line you can draw across a circle, and it is exactly twice as long as a radius. In fig-04 the first circle has its diameter drawn straight across through the centre.

*[figure: Two labelled circles. The first has the diameter drawn across it and a radius from its centre to the circumference, with the arc and the circumference labelled. The second has a chord near the top with the segment above it shaded, a shaded sector below with its right-hand bounding line labelled Radius, and a tangent touching it on the right.]*

Two more parts of a circle are named from the regions the lines cut out of it.

A **segment** is the region of the circle cut off by a chord. In the second circle of fig-04, the chord near the top cuts the circle, and the shaded part above the chord is a segment.

A **sector** is a region of the circle shaped like a slice, bounded by two radii and the arc between them. In fig-04, the second circle shows a shaded sector with two radii as its straight sides and an arc between them. The right-hand side of that sector is labelled Radius.

Finally, the **tangent** is a straight line that touches the circumference at exactly one point and does not cut through the circle. In fig-04, the line on the right-hand side of the second circle touches the circle at a single point — that is a tangent.

**Practice**

1. Name the part of the circle that is the whole curved line forming the circle's outer boundary.

2. A straight line joins two points on the circumference and passes through the centre of the circle. What is this line called?

3. A straight line touches the circumference at exactly one point without cutting through the circle. What is this line called?

4. A straight line joining the centre of a circle to the circumference is called a radius. If the radius of a circle is 4 cm, how long is its diameter?

### The Perimeter of Plane Shapes

The **perimeter** of a shape is the total distance all the way round it. To find it you add up the lengths of every side that makes the boundary of the shape.

For shapes with sides of equal length, you can multiply. A **regular polygon** is a shape whose sides are all the same length, so its perimeter is one side length multiplied by the number of sides.

Here are the forms you will use:

- **Square** — all four sides are equal. Perimeter $= L + L + L + L = 4L$.
- **Rectangle** — two long sides (length $L$) and two short sides (breadth $b$). Perimeter $= L + b + L + b = 2L + 2b$.
- **Trapezium** — four sides, all possibly different. Perimeter $= a + b + c + d$.
- **Parallelogram** — two pairs of equal opposite sides. Perimeter $= 2a + 2b$.
- **Regular polygon** — $n$ equal sides each of length $s$. Perimeter $= n \times s$.
- **Circle** — its perimeter is called the **circumference**. Circumference $= 2\pi r = \pi d$, where $r$ is the radius and $d$ is the diameter, with $d = 2r$.

Because perimeter is a length, every answer is written with a unit such as cm.

**Worked example 1 — square.** Find the perimeter of a square of side 15 cm.

Each side is $L = 15$ cm and there are four of them:
$$P = 4L = 4 \times 15 = 60\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 2 — rectangle.** Find the perimeter of a rectangle of length 10 cm and breadth 7 cm.

$$P = 2L + 2b = 2(10) + 2(7) = 20 + 14 = 34\ \text{cm}.$$
Check: $10 + 7 + 10 + 7 = 34$ cm, the same answer.

**Worked example 3 — rectangle, finding a missing side.** If the perimeter of a rectangle is 60 cm and its length is 17 cm, find its breadth.

Write the perimeter formula and substitute what you know:
$$60 = 2(17) + 2b = 34 + 2b.$$
Collect the like terms:
$$60 - 34 = 2b \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2b = 26.$$
Divide both sides by 2:
$$b = \frac{26}{2} = 13\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 4 — trapezium.** Find the perimeter of a trapezium with sides $a = 11$ cm, $b = 9$ cm, $c = 5$ cm and $d = 4$ cm.

Add all four sides:
$$P = a + b + c + d = 11 + 9 + 5 + 4 = 29\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 5 — parallelogram.** Find the perimeter of a parallelogram with base $a = 9$ cm and slanted side $b = 5$ cm.

The two bases are equal and the two slanted sides are equal:
$$P = 2a + 2b = 2(9) + 2(5) = 18 + 10 = 28\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 6 — regular hexagon.** Find the perimeter of a regular hexagon of side 5.2 cm.

A hexagon has 6 sides, all equal:
$$P = 6 \times 5.2 = 31.2\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 7 — circle.** Calculate the perimeter (circumference) of a circle of radius 7 cm. Use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

Using the radius:
$$C = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 2 \times 22 = 44\ \text{cm}.$$
The same result comes from $\pi d$: since $d = 2r = 14$ cm,
$$C = \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 22 \times 2 = 44\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 8 — circumference, finding the radius.** The circumference of a circle is 440 cm. Find the radius, using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

$$440 = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r = \frac{44r}{7}.$$
Cross-multiply:
$$440 \times 7 = 44r \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3080 = 44r.$$
Divide both sides by 44:
$$r = \frac{3080}{44} = 70\ \text{cm}.$$

**Worked example 9 — a shape made from a quarter circle.** A shape is a quarter circle: two straight sides of 4.9 cm meeting at a right angle, closed by an arc. Find its perimeter.

First find the length of the curved part. It is a quarter of a full circumference with $r = 4.9$ cm:
$$\frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9.$$
Since $2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9 = \frac{44}{7} \times \frac{49}{10} = \frac{44 \times 7}{10} = 30.8$, a quarter of this is $30.8 \div 4 = 7.7$ cm.

Add the two straight sides and the arc:
$$P = 4.9 + 4.9 + 7.7 = 17.5\ \text{cm}.$$

**Naming Plane Shapes and Their Properties**

1. It is a trapezium, and it has no line of symmetry.

   A trapezium is the shape whose only special feature is that it has a single pair of parallel sides. Its properties list shows that it has no line of symmetry.

2. It is a scalene triangle, and no angle is equal to another.

   A triangle with no equal sides is scalene. Its properties are: no side is equal, no angle is equal to another, and it has no line of symmetry.

3. 6 triangles.

   The number of triangles is two fewer than the number of sides. An octagon has 8 sides, so it divides into 8 − 2 = 6 triangles.

4. A square has all four sides equal, while a rectangle has only opposite sides equal (or: a square has four lines of symmetry, a rectangle has two).

   Compare the two rows of the square/rectangle table. The square has all sides equal and four lines of symmetry; the rectangle has opposite sides equal and two lines of symmetry. Either difference is correct.

5. It is a dodecagon, and it divides into 10 triangles.

   From the polygon table, a 12-sided shape is a dodecagon. Its number of triangles is 12 − 2 = 10.

**The Parts of a Circle**

1. The circumference.

2. The diameter (a diameter is any chord that passes through the centre).

3. A tangent.

4. 8 cm, because the diameter is twice the radius.

   The diameter is any chord that passes through the centre, and it is twice as long as a radius. So diameter = 2 × radius = 2 × 4 cm = 8 cm.

**The Perimeter of Plane Shapes**

1. 32 cm

   $P = 4L = 4 \times 8 = 32$ cm.

2. 22 cm

   $P = 2L + 2b = 2(7) + 2(4) = 14 + 8 = 22$ cm.

3. 25 cm

   A pentagon has 5 equal sides, so $P = 5 \times 5 = 25$ cm.

4. 22 cm

   $P = 2a + 2b = 2(6) + 2(5) = 12 + 10 = 22$ cm.

5. 3.5 cm

   $22 = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r = \frac{44r}{7}$. Cross-multiply: $22 \times 7 = 44r$, so $154 = 44r$ and $r = \frac{154}{44} = 3.5$ cm.

6. $6d$

   Length $L = 2d$ and breadth $b = d$. Perimeter $= 2L + 2b = 2(2d) + 2(d) = 4d + 2d = 6d$.

7. 30 cm

   A hexagon has 6 equal sides, so $P = 6 \times 5 = 30$ cm.
