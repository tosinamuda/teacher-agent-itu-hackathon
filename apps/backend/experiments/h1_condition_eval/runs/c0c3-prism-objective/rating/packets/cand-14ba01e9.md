### Constructing Parallel Lines

Parallel lines are lines in the same plane that never meet, however far they are extended. Two things you will use to draw them are a ruler and a set square (the triangle from your mathematical set).

Here is the idea behind the method: a set square has straight edges that meet at right angles. If you slide the set square along a straight edge without turning it, any line you draw along one of its edges will stay at the same angle to the edge you slid along. Lines drawn at the same angle to the same line are parallel to each other.

To draw a line parallel to a given line, follow these steps with your ruler and set square:

1. Draw your given line across your page with the ruler and label it (for example, call it line <em>AB</em>).
2. Place the ruler beside line <em>AB</em> so that one long edge of the ruler lies exactly along the line. Keep the ruler firmly in this position — it is your guide, and it must not move.
3. Rest one edge of the set square against the ruler's edge. The set square should touch the ruler so that its own edges make a right angle with the ruler's edge.
4. Holding the ruler still, slide the set square along the ruler to the place where you want the new parallel line to be.
5. Draw a straight line along the edge of the set square that is furthest from the ruler. That new line is parallel to your given line.

Because the set square slid without turning, its edge drew a line that makes exactly the same angle with the ruler as the given line does. Two lines that make the same angle with a third line are parallel, so your new line is parallel to line <em>AB</em>.

Worked example — draw a line parallel to line <em>CD</em>.

Suppose you have drawn line <em>CD</em> and you want a parallel line about 5 cm below it.

- Place your ruler so that one long edge lies exactly along line <em>CD</em>. Hold it there firmly.
- Set the set square against the ruler's edge, with its edges at a right angle to the ruler.
- Keeping the ruler still, slide the set square down the ruler until it is where you want the new line (about 5 cm below <em>CD</em>).
- Draw along the set square's free edge. Label the new line, for instance <em>EF</em>.

Line <em>EF</em> is parallel to line <em>CD</em>. If you slide the set square to several positions and draw each time, every line you draw will be parallel to <em>CD</em> — and therefore parallel to one another.

**Practice**

1. Using your ruler and set square, draw a line <em>AB</em> across your page. Then construct a second line parallel to <em>AB</em>, about 3 cm above it, and label it <em>PQ</em>.

2. Explain in your own words why, when you slide a set square along a ruler, every line you draw is parallel to the first line you drew.

### Constructing Perpendicular Lines

When two lines cross and the four angles where they meet are all right angles (each exactly 90°), the lines are called <strong>perpendicular</strong>. One quick way to recognise a pair of perpendicular lines is to look for a right-angle corner, like the corner of your set square, sitting neatly in the gap where the two lines meet.

You can construct a perpendicular line with just your set square and ruler. Here is the idea: the set square already has a built-in right angle, so if you line up one edge of that right angle with your given line and draw along the other edge, the new line you draw will meet the given line at a perfect right angle.

Follow these steps to draw a line perpendicular to a given line through a point <em>on</em> that line:

1. Draw a straight line and mark a point <strong>P</strong> on it. This point is where your perpendicular will cross the line.
2. Place your set square so that one edge of its right-angle corner lies exactly along the line, with the corner of the right angle sitting on point P.
3. Holding the set square steady, draw a straight line along the other edge of the right angle, starting from P.
4. The new line now meets the first line at P, and the corner where they meet is a right angle. Your two lines are perpendicular.

To check your work, slide the corner of the set square into the angle between the two lines. If the corner fits exactly with no gap, your lines are perpendicular.

<strong>Worked example:</strong> Construct a line perpendicular to AB at the point P on it.

<em>Step 1.</em> Draw a horizontal line and label its ends A and B. Mark a point P somewhere along AB.

<em>Step 2.</em> Take your set square and place it so that the edge of its right-angle corner lies along AB, with the corner of the right angle resting on point P.

<em>Step 3.</em> Keep the set square firmly in place and draw a straight line upward from P along the other edge of the right-angle corner.

<em>Step 4.</em> Check the corner: the angle between AB and your new line is a right angle, so the new line is perpendicular to AB through P.

**Practice**

1. Draw a horizontal line and mark a point Q on it. Using your set square and ruler, construct a line perpendicular to the horizontal line through Q. What should you check to confirm your construction is correct?

2. Two lines cross at a point, and when you check the corner with your set square it fits exactly with no gap. Are the two lines perpendicular? Explain why.

### Bisecting a Line Segment

To <strong>bisect a line segment</strong> means to cut it into two equal parts. When you bisect a segment, the point where the two equal parts meet is called the <strong>midpoint</strong>. The line that cuts the segment into equal halves crosses it at a right angle, so the construction also gives you a perpendicular through the middle of the segment.

You only need your mathematical set — a ruler and a pair of compasses. The compasses are the key tool here: instead of measuring the segment, you use equal <em>radii</em> (the opening of the compass) to locate the two points that sit exactly the same distance from each end.

Here is the plan you will follow every time:

<ol>
<li>Open your compasses to a radius <strong>greater than half the length</strong> of the segment. Keeping this radius fixed, place the point of the compasses on one end of the segment and draw an arc that passes above and below the segment.</li>
<li>Without changing the compass opening, move the point to the other end of the segment and draw another arc. The two arcs cross each other in two places — one above the segment and one below it.</li>
<li>Using your ruler, join those two crossing points with a straight line. This line passes through the midpoint of the segment and crosses it at a right angle.</li>
<li>The point where your new line meets the original segment is its midpoint, and it divides the segment into two equal parts.</li>
</ol>

The reason the arcs must be drawn with the <em>same</em> opening is that every point on an arc is the same distance from the compass point at the centre. Because you used the same radius from each end, each crossing point is the same distance from both ends — and a point that is equally far from the two ends lies exactly on the perpendicular bisector.

<strong>Worked example:</strong> Bisect a line segment AB that is about 8 cm long.

<ol>
<li>Draw a segment AB, about 8 cm long, with your ruler.</li>
<li>Open your compasses so the opening is a little more than half of AB — about 5 cm for an 8 cm segment.</li>
<li>Place the compass point on A. Draw an arc that curves above AB and another below it, using the same opening.</li>
<li>Move the compass point to B <em>without changing the opening</em>. Draw an arc above and below AB again. The new arcs cross the first pair in two points.</li>
<li>With your ruler, draw a straight line through the two crossing points. This line meets AB at its midpoint and stands at a right angle to it.</li>
<li>Label the midpoint M. You have now bisected AB: AM and MB are equal.</li>
</ol>

To check your work, measure AM and MB with the ruler. If you drew your arcs carefully with a fixed compass opening, the two lengths will be exactly equal.

**Practice**

1. Using only your ruler and compasses, bisect the line segment CD below (draw a segment of any convenient length first). Mark the midpoint and label it M, then write down the two equal parts it creates.

2. A builder wants to find the exact middle of a plank that is 120 cm long, without measuring it with a tape. Which instrument would she use to bisect the plank, and what is the length of each of the two equal parts?

3. When you bisect a line segment, you draw two arcs from each end of the segment. Why must the compass opening stay the same for all four arcs?

### Constructing an Angle of 90°

An angle of 90° is a right angle. Its two arms stand perpendicular to one another, like the corner of a book or the corner of this page. You can build one yourself with a compass and a ruler on any straight line.

Here is the method you will use:
1. Draw a straight line and mark a point on it where you want the 90° angle to sit.
2. Put the compass point on that point and draw an arc that cuts the line at two places, one on each side of your point.
3. Open the compass a little wider. Put the compass point on one of the two cuts and draw an arc above the line. Keep the opening the same, put the point on the other cut, and draw another arc above the line. The two arcs meet at a point above the line.
4. Draw a straight line from your marked point through that meeting point.
5. The angle between this new line and the original line is exactly 90°.

Let us construct a 90° angle at a point P on a straight line.

Step 1 — Draw a straight line and mark P somewhere along it.

Step 2 — Put the compass point on P and draw an arc that crosses the line on both sides of P. Call the two crossings A (on the left) and B (on the right).

Step 3 — Widen the compass a little. Put the point on A and draw an arc above the line. Keeping the compass exactly the same width, put the point on B and draw another arc above the line. The two arcs cross each other at a point C.

Step 4 — Use your ruler to draw a straight line from P up through C.

The angle between PC and the original line is 90°. Mark it with the ∟ symbol.

**Practice**

1. Using your ruler and compass, construct an angle of 90° on a straight line you draw yourself. Mark the point where the angle is made, and label the constructed right angle with the symbol ∟.

2. Look at this construction for an angle at point P: an arc from P cuts the line at A and B, and two wider arcs from A and B meet above the line at C. Without drawing anything else, what is the size of the angle APC?

### Constructing a 60° Angle with a Compass

A 60° angle is one of the angles you can build with just a straight edge and a compass — no protractor needed. The secret is that an equilateral triangle has three equal sides, and every one of its angles is exactly 60°. So if you draw an equilateral triangle, you have built a 60° angle for free.

You will need your mathematical set: a sharp pencil, a ruler (or straight edge), and a compass. Keep the compass setting fixed at the same radius through the whole construction — that is what makes the triangle equilateral.

Here is the whole construction in words before you try it yourself.

1. Draw a straight line and mark a point on it. Call that point A. This will be the vertex of your angle.
2. Set your compass to a comfortable radius. With the compass point on A, draw an arc that crosses the line. Call the point where the arc meets the line B.
3. Keep the compass at exactly the same radius. Now put the compass point on B and draw a second arc that cuts the first arc. Call the point where the two arcs meet C.
4. Use your ruler to join C to A with a straight line.

The line AC makes an angle of 60° with the line AB at A. Done — you have constructed a 60° angle.

Let's build a 60° angle together, step by step.

Start: Draw a line and mark point A on it. Set your compass to a radius of, say, 4 cm — any radius will work, as long as you never change it.

Step 1 — Draw the first arc. With the compass point on A, draw an arc that crosses the line. The point where the arc meets the line is B.

Step 2 — Draw the second arc. Without changing the compass radius, place the point on B and draw another arc so that it crosses the first arc. Where the two arcs meet, mark point C.

Step 3 — Join C to A. Use your ruler to draw the straight line from C back to A.

Now look at triangle ABC. The two arcs used the same radius, so AB = AC = BC. That makes triangle ABC equilateral, and every angle of an equilateral triangle is 60°. So the angle at A, between line AB and line AC, is exactly 60°. The construction is complete.

**Practice**

1. Using only your ruler and compass, construct a 60° angle. Follow the construction: draw a line, mark point A, set a compass radius, draw a first arc to meet the line at B, keep the same radius, draw a second arc from B to cut the first at C, then join C to A. When you finish, measure your angle with a protractor to check it is about 60°.

2. Why must you keep the compass at the same radius when you draw the second arc from point B?
