## The revision pack

### Cylinders and Spheres: Their Properties

Two of the most familiar three-dimensional figures around you are the cylinder and the sphere. A can of drink is a cylinder; a football or a ball of wool is a sphere.

Start with the cylinder. Consider the cylinder in fig-01. A solid cylinder has two circular faces: one on top and one at the bottom. Joining them together around the outside is a single curved face that rolls around the whole body. So altogether a solid cylinder has:

- two circular faces and one curved face, making **three faces** altogether;
- **no vertex** — there is no sharp corner or point anywhere on it;
- **two curved edges**, the two circles where each circular face meets the curved face.

Now consider the sphere in fig-02. A sphere is a perfectly round figure, like a ball. Its properties are simpler still:

- it is **symmetrical from all directions** — however you turn it, it looks the same;
- it has **no edge and no vertex** — there are no straight lines, corners or points on it;
- it has **one curved surface** that covers the whole figure.

The easiest way to tell a cylinder from a sphere is to count the faces and edges: a cylinder has three faces and two curved edges, while a sphere has just one curved surface and nothing to count as an edge or vertex.

### Volume of a cube, a cuboid and a prism

Volume is the amount of space a shape takes up. For a cuboid — a box with a rectangular base — you find the volume by multiplying the length, the breadth and the height:

$$\text{Volume of a cuboid} = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} \times \text{height} = lbh$$

You can also think of it as the area of the base times the height. Since the base is a rectangle of area $l \times b$, multiplying by the height $h$ gives $lbh$.

A cube is a cuboid whose length, breadth and height are all the same. If each side is $l$, then:

$$\text{Volume of a cube} = l \times l \times l = l^3$$

For a prism — a solid with a constant cross-section — the volume is the area of the base (the end face) times the height:

$$\text{Volume of a prism} = \text{area of base} \times \text{height}$$

You use whichever end face is constant, find its area, then multiply by how far the prism runs back.

**Example 1. Find the volume of a cuboid of length 3 cm, breadth 2 cm and height 8 cm.**

$$\text{Volume} = L \times b \times h$$

Length $= 3\text{ cm}$, breadth $= 2\text{ cm}$, height $= 8\text{ cm}$.

$$\text{Volume} = 3\text{ cm} \times 2\text{ cm} \times 8\text{ cm} = 48\text{ cm}^3$$

So the cuboid holds $48\text{ cm}^3$.

---

**Example 2. The volume of a cuboid is $24\text{ cm}^3$. If its base is 6 cm by 2 cm, find its height.**

$$\text{Volume} = L \times b \times h$$

Length $= 6\text{ cm}$, breadth $= 2\text{ cm}$, height $= h$ (unknown).

$$24\text{ cm}^3 = 6\text{ cm} \times 2\text{ cm} \times h$$

$$24\text{ cm}^3 = 12\text{ cm}^2 \times h$$

Divide both sides by $12\text{ cm}^2$:

$$\frac{24\text{ cm}^3}{12\text{ cm}^2} = \frac{12\text{ cm}^2 \times h}{12\text{ cm}^2}$$

$$2\text{ cm} = h$$

So the height is $2\text{ cm}$.

---

**Example 3. Find the volume of a cube of side 3 cm.**

$$\text{Volume of a cube} = l^3$$

$$V = (3\text{ cm})^3 = 3\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm} = 27\text{ cm}^3$$

---

**Example 4. Find the volume of the triangular prism**, where the triangular end has a base of 5 m and a height of 3 m, and the prism runs back 10 m.

First find the area of the triangular base:

$$\text{Area of triangle} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5 \times 3 = 7.5\text{ m}^2$$

Then multiply by the length of the prism (10 m):

$$\text{Volume of prism} = \text{area of base} \times \text{height} = 7.5\text{ m}^2 \times 10\text{ m} = 75\text{ m}^3$$

## Check your work

**Cylinders and Spheres: Their Properties**

1. A solid cylinder has two circular faces (one on top and one at the bottom).

   Look at a solid cylinder: the top is one circle and the bottom is another circle, so there are two circular faces in total.

2. A cylinder has three faces altogether: two circular faces and one curved face.

   Count them: one circular face on top, one circular face at the bottom, and the curved face wrapping around the outside. That gives 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 faces.

3. No. A sphere has no edge and no vertex — it is one continuous curved surface with no corners or straight edges.

   A sphere is perfectly round, like a ball. Because it is smooth all over, there is no straight line (edge) and no corner or point (vertex) anywhere on it.

**Volume of a cube, a cuboid and a prism**

1. $8 \times 4 \times 3 = 96\text{ cm}^3$

   Volume of a cuboid $= L \times b \times h = 8\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} \times 3\text{ cm} = 96\text{ cm}^3$.

2. $5 \times 2 \times 3 = 30\text{ m}^3$

   Volume of a cuboid $= L \times b \times h = 5\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m} = 30\text{ m}^3$.

3. $4^3 = 64\text{ cm}^3$

   Volume of a cube $= l^3 = (4\text{ cm})^3 = 4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64\text{ cm}^3$.

4. 3 m

   Volume $= L \times b \times h$, so $36\text{ m}^3 = 6\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} \times h = 12\text{ m}^2 \times h$. Divide both sides by $12\text{ m}^2$: $h = \frac{36\text{ m}^3}{12\text{ m}^2} = 3\text{ m}$.
