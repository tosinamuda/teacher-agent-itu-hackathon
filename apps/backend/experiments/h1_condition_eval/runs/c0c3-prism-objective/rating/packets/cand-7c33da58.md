### Cubes and Cuboids: Faces, Edges and Vertices

Solid shapes that are not flat are called <b>three-dimensional (3D) shapes</b> because they have length, width and height. Two 3D shapes you meet everywhere are the cube and the cuboid.

Think of a dice, a Rubik's cube or a box of salt — these are all 3D shapes. To describe any 3D shape, you use three special parts:

<ul>
<li>A <b>face</b> is a flat surface of the shape. When you feel the top of a box, you are touching one of its faces.</li>
<li>An <b>edge</b> is a straight line where two faces meet. Run your finger along the corner of a book and you are tracing an edge.</li>
<li>A <b>vertex</b> (plural <b>vertices</b>) is a corner, where three or more edges meet. The sharp points of a box are its vertices.</li>
</ul>

Now let us look closely at a cube. A cube is a special 3D shape where <b>every face is a square</b> and <b>every edge is the same length</b>. Because all its faces are equal squares, a cube looks exactly the same no matter which face you look at.

A cube has these properties:
<ul>
<li><b>6 faces</b> — all of them squares and all of them the same size.</li>
<li><b>12 edges</b> — all the same length.</li>
<li><b>8 vertices</b> — every corner where three edges meet.</li>
</ul>

A cuboid, sometimes called a rectangular prism or a rectangular box, is like a stretched cube. Its faces are <b>rectangles</b> (though some may be squares), and opposite faces are equal to each other. A shoe box or a book is a cuboid.

A cuboid has these properties:
<ul>
<li><b>6 faces</b> — they are rectangles; the two opposite faces are equal to each other.</li>
<li><b>12 edges</b> — but not all the same length; edges come in sets of four equal ones.</li>
<li><b>8 vertices</b>.</li>
</ul>

Notice the important difference: both a cube and a cuboid have <b>6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices</b>. The difference is in their faces. Every face of a cube is a square, while the faces of a cuboid are rectangles (only some of them might be squares).

<b>Worked Example 1: Describing a cube</b>

A student picks up a die. How many faces, edges and vertices does it have, and what shape are its faces?

<b>Solution:</b>
A die is a cube. A cube has <b>6 faces</b>, <b>12 edges</b> and <b>8 vertices</b>. Since every face of a cube is a square, the faces are all <b>squares</b>, and they are all the same size.

<b>Worked Example 2: Cube or cuboid?</b>

Look at these two boxes.
<ul>
<li>Box A is a salt container with all its sides the same length and square faces.</li>
<li>Box B is a shoe box with rectangular faces, where opposite faces match.</li>
</ul>
Which one is a cube and which one is a cuboid?

<b>Solution:</b>
Box A has equal edges and square faces, so it is a <b>cube</b>. Box B has rectangular faces where only opposite faces are equal, so it is a <b>cuboid</b>.

<b>Worked Example 3: Counting edges on a cuboid</b>

A cuboid has faces that come in three pairs of equal rectangles. How many edges does it have, and how do the edges group?

<b>Solution:</b>
A cuboid has <b>12 edges</b>. They are not all equal. There are four edges along the length, four along the width and four along the height. So the 12 edges come in three sets of 4 equal edges.

**Practice**

1. A Rubik's cube is a cube. How many faces, edges and vertices does it have, and what shape are all its faces?

2. State whether each shape is a cube or a cuboid, and give one reason for your choice.
   (a) A gift box where every face is a rectangle and opposite faces are equal.
   (b) A block where every face is a square and every edge is the same length.

3. A cuboid has 6 faces and 8 vertices. How many edges does it have? Explain whether all these edges are the same length.

### Pyramids and Cones: Faces, Edges and Vertices

We have looked at cubes and cuboids, where every face is flat and every face is a rectangle or square. Pyramids and cones are different: they rise up to a single point. That point is called the <strong>apex</strong> (you can also call it the vertex at the top).

<h3>Pyramids</h3>
A pyramid has one flat <strong>base</strong>, which can be any polygon (a triangle, a square, a pentagon, and so on). Every other face of a pyramid is a triangle, and all those triangles meet together at the apex above the base.

To count the parts of a pyramid, look at the shape of its base. Suppose the base has <em>n</em> sides:
<ul>
  <li><strong>Faces:</strong> the base is 1 face, plus one triangular face for each side of the base, so the total is <em>n</em> + 1 faces.</li>
  <li><strong>Edges:</strong> the base has <em>n</em> edges around its rim, and each of those base corners sends one sloping edge up to the apex, giving another <em>n</em> edges. Total is 2<em>n</em> edges.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertices:</strong> the base has <em>n</em> corners, plus the apex at the top, giving <em>n</em> + 1 vertices.</li>
</ul>

For example, a <strong>square-based pyramid</strong> has a square base, so <em>n</em> = 4. That gives 4 + 1 = <strong>5 faces</strong>, 2 × 4 = <strong>8 edges</strong>, and 4 + 1 = <strong>5 vertices</strong>.

<h3>Cones</h3>
A cone is like a pyramid, but its base is a <strong>circle</strong>. A cone has:
<ul>
  <li><strong>Faces:</strong> one flat circular base and one curved surface that tapers to the apex. That is <strong>2 faces</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Edge:</strong> one edge — the circular rim where the base meets the curved surface.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertices:</strong> just the apex point at the top, so <strong>1 vertex</strong>.</li>
</ul>

Notice the difference: a pyramid's sloping sides are flat triangles, while a cone's sloping surface is curved and smooth, with no corners along the sides.

<strong>Example 1 — A triangular-based pyramid</strong>
A triangular-based pyramid (also called a tetrahedron) has a triangular base, so <em>n</em> = 3.
<ul>
  <li>Faces: <em>n</em> + 1 = 3 + 1 = <strong>4 faces</strong> (the triangular base plus three triangular sloping faces).</li>
  <li>Edges: 2<em>n</em> = 2 × 3 = <strong>6 edges</strong>.</li>
  <li>Vertices: <em>n</em> + 1 = 3 + 1 = <strong>4 vertices</strong>.</li>
</ul>

<strong>Example 2 — A cone</strong>
A cone has a circular base, so it is not counted with the formula above (a circle has no corners).
<ul>
  <li>Faces: <strong>2</strong> (one circular base and one curved surface).</li>
  <li>Edges: <strong>1</strong> (the circular rim around the base).</li>
  <li>Vertices: <strong>1</strong> (the apex at the top).</li>
</ul>

**Practice**

1. A square-based pyramid has a square base. How many faces, edges, and vertices does it have?

2. A hexagonal-based pyramid has a hexagonal base (a base with 6 sides). Find the number of faces, edges, and vertices of this pyramid.

3. State the number of faces, edges, and vertices of a cone.

**Cubes and Cuboids: Faces, Edges and Vertices**

1. 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices; all faces are squares.

   A Rubik's cube is a cube, so it has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices. Because every face of a cube is a square, all 6 faces are squares and they are all the same size.

2. (a) Cuboid — its faces are rectangles with opposite faces equal. (b) Cube — every face is a square and every edge is the same length.

   For (a), the faces are rectangles (not all squares) and only opposite faces match, which is the property of a cuboid. For (b), every face is a square and all edges are equal, which is exactly the property of a cube.

3. 12 edges. They are not all the same length; they come in three sets of 4 equal edges (length, width and height).

   A cuboid, like a cube, has 12 edges. But unlike a cube, the edges are not all equal: there are 4 edges of one length, 4 of another and 4 of a third, matching the three pairs of opposite faces.

**Pyramids and Cones: Faces, Edges and Vertices**

1. 5 faces, 8 edges, 5 vertices.

   The base is a square, so n = 4. Faces = n + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5. Edges = 2n = 2 × 4 = 8. Vertices = n + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5.

2. 7 faces, 12 edges, 7 vertices.

   The base has n = 6 sides. Faces = n + 1 = 6 + 1 = 7. Edges = 2n = 2 × 6 = 12. Vertices = n + 1 = 6 + 1 = 7.

3. 2 faces, 1 edge, 1 vertex.

   A cone has one flat circular base and one curved surface: that is 2 faces. The circular rim where the base meets the curved surface is its 1 edge. Its only vertex is the apex at the top.
