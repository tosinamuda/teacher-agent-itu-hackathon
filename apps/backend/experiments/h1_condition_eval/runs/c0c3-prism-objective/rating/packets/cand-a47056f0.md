### Cylinders and Spheres, and Finding Volume

Solid shapes are all around you — tins, balls, boxes, and bricks. In this section you will get to know two of them well (the cylinder and the sphere), and then find the volume of three solid shapes: the cube, the cuboid and the prism.

<b>Cylinders</b><br>A cylinder looks like a tin of tomatoes or a drinking can. Its key properties are:<br>• It has two flat circular faces — one at the top and one at the bottom — and they are the same size.<br>• It has one curved surface that joins the two circles.<br>• If you cut it straight across, the slice is always a circle the same size (its cross-section is a constant circle).<br>• It has no vertices (corners).<br>• Its height is the distance between the two circular faces.<br><br><b>Spheres</b><br>A sphere looks like a ball — a football or a marble. Its key properties are:<br>• It is perfectly round.<br>• Every point on its surface is the same distance from its centre. That distance is called the <b>radius</b>.<br>• It has no flat faces, no edges and no vertices at all.<br>• No matter how you slice it, a cut gives you a circle.

<b>Volume</b><br>Volume measures how much space a solid takes up. You work it out by multiplying a shape's length, width and height, and you answer in cubic units (like cm³, meaning centimetres cubed).<br><br>• <b>Cube</b> — all its edges are equal. Volume = side × side × side, or side³.<br>• <b>Cuboid</b> — a box with three different lengths. Volume = length × width × height.<br>• <b>Prism</b> — a shape with a cross-section that is the same all the way through (for example, a triangular prism). Volume = area of the cross-section × length.

<b>Worked Example 1 — the volume of a cube</b><br>Find the volume of a cube whose side is 4 cm.<br><br>Volume of a cube = side × side × side<br>= 4 × 4 × 4<br>= 64 cm³<br><br>So the cube takes up 64 cubic centimetres of space.

<b>Worked Example 2 — the volume of a cuboid</b><br>A cuboid is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide and 2 cm high. Find its volume.<br><br>Volume of a cuboid = length × width × height<br>= 5 × 3 × 2<br>= 30 cm³<br><br>The order of the three measurements does not matter — you always multiply them together.

<b>Worked Example 3 — the volume of a prism</b><br>A triangular prism has a triangular cross-section with area 12 cm², and its length is 6 cm. Find its volume.<br><br>Volume of a prism = area of cross-section × length<br>= 12 × 6<br>= 72 cm³<br><br>The prism holds 72 cubic centimetres.

### Cylinders, Spheres, and Finding Volume

Before we measure shapes, let's make sure we know them. A <strong>cylinder</strong> is like a tin or a drum: it has two flat, circular faces (top and bottom) that are the same size and face each other, and one curved surface that joins them. Its two circular faces are identical and lie flat and parallel to each other. A <strong>sphere</strong> is perfectly round like a ball or an orange: it has just one surface, the whole outside is curved, and there is no flat face, no edge and no corner. From the centre of a sphere, every point on its surface is exactly the same distance away.</p>
<p>Now for the main job of this section: finding <strong>volume</strong>. The volume of a solid is the amount of space it takes up, and we measure it in cubic units such as cm<sup>3</sup> or m<sup>3</sup>. For a box-shaped solid, the volume is found by multiplying its three measurements together.</p>
<p>A <strong>cube</strong> is a box where every edge is the same length. If one edge is length <em>s</em>, then:</p>
<p style="text-align:center"><strong>Volume of a cube = s × s × s = s<sup>3</sup></strong></p>
<p>A <strong>cuboid</strong> is a box where the faces are rectangles, and its three edges can all be different lengths — length <em>l</em>, breadth <em>b</em> and height <em>h</em>. Then:</p>
<p style="text-align:center"><strong>Volume of a cuboid = l × b × h</strong></p>
<p>Many solids are <strong>prisms</strong>. A prism is a solid with the same cross-section all the way through — think of a Toblerone bar, a pipe, or a wedge of cheese. For any prism:</p>
<p style="text-align:center"><strong>Volume of a prism = area of cross-section × length</strong></p>
<p>So once you can work out the area of the shape at the end, you multiply it by how long the prism is.</p>

<strong>Example 1 — Volume of a cube.</strong> A cube has edges of length 4 cm. Find its volume.<br>
Volume = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. So the volume is <strong>64 cm<sup>3</sup></strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Example 2 — Volume of a cuboid.</strong> A box measures 5 cm long, 3 cm broad and 2 cm high. Find its volume.<br>
Volume = 5 × 3 × 2 = 30. So the volume is <strong>30 cm<sup>3</sup></strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Example 3 — Volume of a prism.</strong> A prism has a triangular end with an area of 8 cm<sup>2</sup>, and it is 6 cm long. Find its volume.<br>
Volume = area of cross-section × length = 8 × 6 = 48. So the volume is <strong>48 cm<sup>3</sup></strong>.</p>
<p>Notice the pattern: a cube and a cuboid are actually special prisms, because their cross-section is the same all the way through. For a cuboid, that cross-section is a rectangle, so its area is length × breadth, and multiplying by the height again gives length × breadth × height.</p>
