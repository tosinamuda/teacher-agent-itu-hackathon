## The revision pack

### Cubes and Cuboids: Faces, Edges and Vertices

A **three dimensional shape** has length, breadth and height, so it takes up space. That is why these shapes are also called **solid shapes**. The cube and the cuboid are two of these solid shapes. Before we count their parts, let us fix three words you will need.

- A **face** (or surface) is one of the sides of the shape. A face may be flat or curved.
- An **edge** is a line where two faces meet. It may be straight or curved.
- A **vertex** is a point corner where edges meet. The plural of vertex is **vertices**.

**The cube.** A cube is a solid shape whose faces are all equal squares. Look at **fig-01**: a cube drawn as a see-through solid, with one square offset from another and their corners joined. Count its parts carefully.

- It has **6 square faces** — the six equal squares that cover it.
- It has **12 edges** — the straight lines where the faces meet.
- It has **8 vertices** — the 8 point corners where the edges meet.

A cube is a **regular** solid shape: its faces are all the same shape and size.

*[figure: A cube drawn as a see-through solid: two equal squares, the nearer one offset down and to the right of the farther one, with the four pairs of corresponding corners joined.]*

**The cuboid.** A cuboid is also a solid shape with six faces, but its faces are rectangles rather than equal squares. In **fig-02** the cuboid is drawn wide and shallow, and three leader lines name its parts: the top **face**, a **vertex** (the point corner), and an **edge** (the line where two faces meet).

Count its parts in the same way:

- It has **6 faces**.
- It has **12 edges**.
- It has **8 vertices**.

The cube and the cuboid share the same numbers of faces, edges and vertices. What makes them different is that every face of a cube is an equal square, while a cuboid's faces are rectangles (usually of two or three different sizes).

*[figure: A cuboid drawn the same way, wide and shallow, with three leader lines to the right naming the top face, the nearer top-right corner and the nearer right-hand vertical edge.]*

**Practice**

1. How many vertices has a cuboid?

2. How many edges has a cube?

3. How many square faces has a cube, and how are a cube's faces different from a cuboid's faces?

### Pyramids and Cones: Faces, Edges and Vertices

A pyramid is named by the shape of its base. The base is the flat face it stands on, and from every corner of that base one straight edge runs up to a single point at the top, called the apex. Look at the triangular base pyramid in fig-03: its base is a triangle, so it is a triangular based pyramid. Its special name is a tetrahedron.

The square based pyramid in fig-04, the pentagonal based pyramid in fig-05 and the rectangular based pyramid in fig-06 are all named the same way — by what their base is.

Every pyramid has faces, edges and vertices, and you can count them. Remember: a face is a flat surface, an edge is where two faces meet, and a vertex is a corner where edges meet. Here is how the count works out:

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triangular based pyramid | 4 | 6 | 4 |
| Square based pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |
| Pentagonal based pyramid | 6 | 10 | 6 |
| Rectangular based pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |

A cone (fig-07) is different. It is round, so it has a flat circular base and a curved face that narrows up to one point. Altogether a cone has two faces — one curved face and one flat face — one edge (the rim of the circular base), and one vertex (the point at the top).

*[figure: Two drawings side by side. On the left an upright cone: an elliptical base with the two slant sides running up from the ends of the ellipse to the apex. On the right a cone lying on its side, apex at the left, its two slant sides reaching a narrow upright ellipse whose top and bottom then continue as two parallel lines to a second narrow ellipse further right.]*

*[figure: A rectangular based pyramid, drawn like the square one but on a base wider than it is tall.]*

*[figure: A pentagonal based pyramid. The base is drawn as a pentagon standing on one side with its fifth vertex uppermost, and the apex above is joined to that vertex, to the two vertices either side of it, and to the lower-right vertex — four edges from the apex, the last of them crossing the face of the solid.]*

*[figure: A square base pyramid. The base is drawn as an upright rectangle and the apex above is joined to all four of its corners, so two edges run outside the rectangle and two cross it.]*

*[figure: A triangular base pyramid. The base triangle is drawn in perspective, its third vertex raised into the middle of the figure, and the apex above is joined to all three base vertices.]*

Let's name a solid and then count its parts.

Example 1 — Name the solid with a square base. Its base is a square, so it is a square based pyramid. From the table, a square based pyramid has 5 faces, 8 edges and 5 vertices.

Example 2 — How many edges does a pentagonal based pyramid have? Its base is a pentagon (five sides). Reading the row for the pentagonal based pyramid: 6 faces, 10 edges, 6 vertices. So it has 10 edges.

Example 3 — A cone has a curved face and a flat circular base. The flat base meets the curved face along one circular rim. So a cone has 2 faces, 1 edge and 1 vertex.

**Practice**

1. Draw a hexagonal base pyramid (a pyramid whose base has six sides) and list its properties. How many faces, edges and vertices does it have?

2. How many vertices does a tetrahedron have? What kind of base does it have, and how is it named?

3. How many faces, edges and vertices does a cone have?

## Check your work

**Cubes and Cuboids: Faces, Edges and Vertices**

1. A cuboid has 8 vertices.

2. A cube has 12 edges.

3. A cube has 6 square faces. A cube's faces are all equal squares, while a cuboid's faces are rectangles (so they are not all equal squares).

**Pyramids and Cones: Faces, Edges and Vertices**

1. A hexagonal based pyramid has 7 faces, 12 edges and 7 vertices.

   The pyramid is named by its base, so a hexagonal base gives a hexagonal based pyramid. Its base is a hexagon (6 sides). A pyramid with a base of n sides has n + 1 faces (the n triangular side faces plus the base), 2n edges, and n + 1 vertices. With n = 6: 6 + 1 = 7 faces, 2 × 6 = 12 edges, and 6 + 1 = 7 vertices.

2. A tetrahedron has 4 vertices. It has a triangular base, so it is also called a triangular based pyramid.

   A tetrahedron is a triangular based pyramid, and the triangular based pyramid row of the table shows 4 faces, 6 edges and 4 vertices — so it has 4 vertices.

3. A cone has 2 faces (one curved face and one flat circular base), 1 edge and 1 vertex.
