## The revision pack

### Types of Plane Shapes and Their Properties

Plane shapes, also called two-dimensional (2-D) shapes, are flat shapes that lie completely in one plane. They include the triangle, the rectangle, the square, the parallelogram, the trapezium, the circle and all the other polygons.

Let's begin with the triangle, a closed shape with three straight sides. Triangles are named by how many of their sides are equal, as fig-01 shows. Look at the little ticks on the sides: sides with the same number of ticks are equal in length.

• An <strong>isosceles</strong> triangle has two equal sides (one tick on each of the two slanted sides). Its base angles are equal, and it has one line of symmetry.
• A <strong>scalene</strong> triangle has no equal sides (one, two and three ticks on its three sides). No angle is equal to another, and it has no line of symmetry.
• An <strong>equilateral</strong> triangle has three equal sides (one tick on each of the three sides). All its angles are equal, each measuring 60°, and it has three lines of symmetry.

*[figure: Isosceles: a single tick on each of the two slanted sides, an arc at each base angle. Scalene: one, two and three ticks on the three sides, an arc at each vertex. Equilateral: a single tick on each of the three sides, an arc at each vertex.]*

Next come the square and the rectangle, shown in fig-02. The right-angle marks at each corner tell you that every corner is a 90° angle.

The <strong>square</strong>:
• all four sides are equal,
• it has two pairs of parallel sides,
• all its angles are equal (90° each),
• it has four lines of symmetry,
• its diagonals bisect it at a right angle.

The <strong>rectangle</strong>:
• only opposite sides are equal (the two longer sides carry one tick, the two shorter sides carry two ticks),
• it has two pairs of parallel sides,
• all its angles are equal (90° each),
• it has two lines of symmetry,
• its diagonals bisect it, but not at a right angle.

Notice the similarity: both are quadrilaterals whose angles are all 90°. The difference is that a square's four sides are all equal, while a rectangle only needs its opposite sides equal.

*[figure: Square: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the four sides. Rectangle: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the two longer sides and a double tick on each of the two shorter sides.]*

Now the parallelogram and the trapezium, in fig-03. The arrows on the sides mark which sides are parallel: one arrow means one pair of parallel sides, two arrows mark the other pair.

The <strong>parallelogram</strong>:
• its two pairs of opposite sides are equal and parallel,
• opposite angles are equal,
• it has no line of symmetry,
• its diagonals divide it into equal triangles.

The <strong>trapezium</strong>:
• only a pair of sides are parallel,
• opposite angles are not equal,
• it has no line of symmetry,
• its diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles.

Here is the key difference: a parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides, while a trapezium has only one pair.

*[figure: Parallelogram: a single arrow on each of the two horizontal sides, a double arrow on each of the two slanted sides. Trapezium: a single arrow on each of the two parallel sides, and one diagonal drawn in.]*

Finally, a <strong>polygon</strong> is a closed shape with three or more straight sides. Each polygon has a name that comes from its number of sides, and every polygon with n sides can be divided into n − 2 triangles by drawing diagonals from one vertex. Here is the list to learn:

| Name of polygon | Number of sides | Number of triangles (n − 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Triangle | 3 | 1 |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 2 |
| Pentagon | 5 | 3 |
| Hexagon | 6 | 4 |
| Heptagon | 7 | 5 |
| Octagon | 8 | 6 |
| Nonagon | 9 | 7 |
| Decagon | 10 | 8 |
| Hendecagon (undecagon) | 11 | 9 |
| Dodecagon | 12 | 10 |

The rule n − 2 works for every polygon. A hexagon has 6 sides, so it splits into 6 − 2 = 4 triangles; a dodecagon has 12 sides, so it splits into 12 − 2 = 10 triangles.

**Practice**

1. How many triangles can an octagon be divided into if you draw diagonals from one vertex to all non-adjacent vertices?

2. Name the triangle in which all three sides are equal, and state the size of each of its angles.

3. A shape has two pairs of parallel sides, all its angles are 90° each, but only its opposite sides are equal. Is it a square or a rectangle? Explain.

4. State one difference between a parallelogram and a trapezium.

5. A nonagon has how many sides, and into how many triangles can it be divided?

### Parts of a Circle

A circle is a special kind of plane shape, and to talk about it clearly we give names to its different parts. Look at the two labelled circles in fig-04 as you read.

The **circumference** is the curved boundary of the circle — the round line that goes all the way around it. Any curved part of that circumference is called an **arc**.

Inside the circle, draw a straight line from the very centre of the circle to the circumference. That line is the **radius** (more than one are called radii). Every radius of the same circle is the same length.

Now draw a straight line joining any two points on the circumference. That line is called a **chord**. A chord cuts across the inside of the circle. When a chord passes through the centre of the circle, it is given a special name — the **diameter**. So the diameter is just a chord that goes through the centre, and it is the longest chord of the circle. Notice that a diameter is two radii put end to end.

Two more parts are shown in the second circle. The region between a chord and the arc above it is called a **segment** (in fig-04 it is the shaded part above the chord). The region between two radii and the arc between them — a slice of the circle, like a piece of cake — is called a **sector**. Finally, a **tangent** is a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point and does not cut across it.

*[figure: Two labelled circles. The first has the diameter drawn across it and a radius from its centre to the circumference, with the arc and the circumference labelled. The second has a chord near the top with the segment above it shaded, a shaded sector below with its right-hand bounding line labelled Radius, and a tangent touching it on the right.]*

**Practice**

1. A straight line is drawn from the centre of a circle to a point on the circumference. What is this line called?

2. Which part of a circle is a straight line joining two points on the circumference that passes through the centre?

3. What do we call a curved part of the circumference of a circle?

4. A straight line touches a circle at exactly one point without crossing it. What is it called?

5. A diameter of a circle is 8 cm long. How long is the radius of that circle?

### The Perimeter of Plane Shapes

The perimeter of a shape is the distance all the way round it. To find it, you add up the lengths of all the sides. Because you already know angles, you can recognise each shape's special feature that makes finding its perimeter quicker: a square has four equal sides, a rectangle has two equal lengths and two equal breadths, and so on.

<strong>Square.</strong> A square has four equal sides. If one side is $L$, the perimeter is the sum of all four:

$$L + L + L + L = 4L$$

So the perimeter of a square is $4 \times$ (length of one side).

<strong>Example 1.</strong> Calculate the perimeter of a square of side 15cm.

<em>fig-05</em> shows a square with all four sides 15cm.

Since the four sides are equal,

$$\text{Perimeter} = L + L + L + L = 4L$$

$$\phantom{\text{Perimeter}} = 15 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 4(15)$$

$$\phantom{\text{Perimeter}} = 60\mathrm{cm}$$

So the square's perimeter is 60cm.

*[figure: A square, named *Square* at its left, with all four sides marked twice — $L$ and 15cm.]*

<strong>Rectangle.</strong> A rectangle has two equal lengths $L$ and two equal breadths $b$. Adding all four sides:

$$L + b + L + b = 2L + 2b = 2(L + b)$$

So the perimeter of a rectangle is twice the sum of its length and breadth.

<strong>Example 2.</strong> Find the perimeter of a rectangle whose length is 10cm and breadth 7cm.

<em>fig-06</em> shows a rectangle with length $L = 10\mathrm{cm}$ and breadth $b = 7\mathrm{cm}$.

Adding the four sides:

$$L + b + L + b = 10 + 7 + 10 + 7$$

Rearranging,

$$= 10 + 10 + 7 + 7 = 2(10) + 2(7)$$

$$= 2(10 + 7) = 2(17)$$

$$= 34\mathrm{cm}$$

The rectangle's perimeter is 34cm.

*[figure: A rectangle with both long sides marked $L$, the top one also 10cm, and both short sides marked $b$, the right-hand one also 7cm.]*

<strong>Example 3.</strong> If the perimeter of a rectangle is 60cm and its length is 17cm, calculate its breadth.

<em>fig-07</em> shows a rectangle with length $L = 17\mathrm{cm}$ and unknown breadth $b$.

Using $\text{Perimeter} = L + b + L + b$,

$$60 = 17 + b + 17 + b$$

$$60 = 17 + 17 + b + b = 2(17) + 2b$$

$$60 = 34 + 2b$$

Collect the like terms:

$$60 - 34 = 2b$$

$$2b = 26$$

Divide both sides by 2:

$$\frac{2b}{2} = \frac{26}{2}$$

$$b = 13\mathrm{cm}$$

The breadth of the rectangle is 13cm.

*[figure: A rectangle with both long sides marked $L = 17$cm and both short sides marked $b$.]*

<strong>Trapezium.</strong> A trapezium has four sides of no particular equal lengths, so its perimeter is simply the sum of all four sides.

<strong>Parallelogram.</strong> A parallelogram has two pairs of equal sides: two equal sides $a$ and two equal sides $b$. Its perimeter is therefore

$$a + a + b + b = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b)$$

<strong>Example 4.</strong> Find the perimeter of the trapezium below.

<em>fig-08</em> shows a trapezium with sides $a = 11\mathrm{cm}$, $b = 9\mathrm{cm}$, $c = 5\mathrm{cm}$ and $d = 4\mathrm{cm}$.

Adding the four sides:

$$\text{Perimeter} = a + b + c + d$$

$$= 11 + 9 + 5 + 4$$

$$= 29\mathrm{cm}$$

The trapezium's perimeter is 29cm.

*[figure: A trapezium with each side lettered and measured: the top $c$, 5cm; the left $b$, 9cm; the right $d$, 4cm; the base $a$, 11cm.]*

<strong>Example 5.</strong> Find the perimeter of the parallelogram below.

<em>fig-09</em> shows a parallelogram with sides $a = 9\mathrm{cm}$ and $b = 5\mathrm{cm}$, each side occurring twice.

$$\text{Perimeter} = a + a + b + b = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b)$$

$$= 2(9 + 5) = 2(14)$$

$$= 28\mathrm{cm}$$

The parallelogram's perimeter is 28cm.

*[figure: A parallelogram with a single arrow on each of its four sides: the two horizontal ones both lettered $a$, the lower also measured 9cm, and the two slanted ones both lettered $b$, the right-hand one also measured 5cm.]*

<strong>Regular polygon.</strong> A <em>regular</em> polygon has all its sides equal. A regular hexagon has 6 sides, a regular pentagon has 5 sides. So the perimeter of a regular polygon is

$$\text{(number of sides)} \times \text{(length of one side)}.$$

<strong>Example 6.</strong> Find the perimeter of a regular hexagon with sides 5.2cm.

<em>fig-10</em> shows a regular hexagon with all six sides 5.2cm.

A hexagon has 6 sides, all equal:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 6 \times 5.2\mathrm{cm}$$

$$= 31.2\mathrm{cm}$$

The hexagon's perimeter is 31.2cm.

*[figure: A regular hexagon, named above the drawing, with each of its six sides marked 5.2cm.]*

<strong>Circle.</strong> The perimeter of a circle has its own name: the <em>circumference</em>. If the circle has radius $r$ and diameter $d = 2r$, then

$$\text{Circumference} = 2\pi r \quad \text{or} \quad \pi d.$$

You can use either formula; choose the one that fits what you know.

<strong>Example 7.</strong> Calculate the perimeter of a circle of radius 7cm. Use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

<em>fig-11</em> shows a circle with radius 7cm.

Using $\text{Perimeter} = 2\pi r$:

$$= 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7$$

$$= 2 \times 22$$

$$= 44\mathrm{cm}$$

We can check with $\pi d$. Since $r = 7\mathrm{cm}$, the diameter $d = 2 \times 7 = 14\mathrm{cm}$, and

$$\frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 22 \times 2 = 44\mathrm{cm}.$$

The circumference is 44cm.

*[figure: A circle with a radius drawn from the centre to the right of the circumference, marked 7cm.]*

<strong>Example 8.</strong> The circumference of a circle is 440cm. Find its radius. Use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

<em>fig-13</em> shows a circle with unknown radius $r$.

Using $\text{Circumference} = 2\pi r$:

$$440 = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r$$

$$440 = \frac{44r}{7}$$

Cross multiply:

$$440 \times 7 = 44r$$

$$3080 = 44r$$

Divide both sides by 44:

$$\frac{44r}{44} = \frac{3080}{44}$$

$$r = 70\mathrm{cm}$$

The radius of the circle is 70cm.

*[figure: A circle with a radius drawn from the centre to the right of the circumference, marked $r$.]*

<strong>Example 9.</strong> A rope is wound 50 times round a cylinder of radius 25cm. How long is the rope? Use $\pi = 3.14$.

<em>fig-12</em> shows the rope wound round the cylinder. One complete turn of the rope is the circumference of the cylinder, so the length of rope in one turn is the circumference.

$$\text{Circumference} = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times 25$$

$$= 157\mathrm{cm}$$

The rope goes round 50 times, so the total length of rope is

$$157\mathrm{cm} \times 50 = 7850\mathrm{cm}.$$

The rope is 7850cm long.

*[figure: An upright cylinder with a rope wound round it, drawn as a series of turns spiralling down the body from the top face to the base.]*

<strong>Combined shapes.</strong> Some shapes are made of straight sides and a curved arc. For these, find the length of the curved part and add it to the straight sides. A quarter circle is one quarter of a full circle, so its curved length is

$$\frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r.$$

<strong>Example 10.</strong> Calculate the perimeter of the shape below.

<em>fig-14</em> shows a quarter circle: an upright side and a base, each 4.9cm, joined by an arc.

First find the length of the curved part. The arc is a quarter of a circle of radius 4.9cm:

$$\text{curved length} = \frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9$$

$$= \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{44}{7} \times 4.9 = \frac{11}{7} \times \frac{49}{10}$$

$$= \frac{11 \times 7}{10} = \frac{77}{10} = 7.7\mathrm{cm}$$

Now add the two straight sides:

$$\text{Perimeter} = 4.9 + 4.9 + 7.7 = 17.5\mathrm{cm}$$

The perimeter of the shape is 17.5cm.

*[figure: A quarter circle: an upright side and a base meeting at a right angle, each marked 4.9cm, with an arc closing them from the top of the upright to the end of the base.]*
