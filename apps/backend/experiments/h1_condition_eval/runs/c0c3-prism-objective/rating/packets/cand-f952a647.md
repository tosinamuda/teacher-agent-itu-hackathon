## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is For

Every day you gather facts and figures around you — how many students were in class, how many bottles of water were sold, how many goals your favourite team scored. These facts and figures are called <strong>data</strong>. Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose.

<strong>Statistics</strong> is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data. When you count the pupils present in the morning and write their names in a register, you are collecting data. When you sort those names by class or by age, you are organising it. Statistics brings a clear order to information so that it becomes useful to you.

Why do we bother with statistics at all? Statistics helps us in five important ways:

- <strong>(a) Collect information</strong> — gather the facts you need.
- <strong>(b) Organise information</strong> — put the facts into a sensible order.
- <strong>(c) Compare information</strong> — see how one set of facts stands against another.
- <strong>(d) Make decisions</strong> — use the facts to choose sensibly.
- <strong>(e) Present information clearly</strong> — show the facts so others can understand them at a glance.

Consider a shopkeeper who sells oranges. Each day she counts how many oranges she sells (collecting information), and writes the daily totals in her notebook one after another (organising information). By looking at her notes she can compare Saturday's sales with Monday's (comparing information), and then she decides how many oranges to buy for next weekend (making decisions). When she wants to show her progress to her boss, she draws a simple table or chart so the figures are clear at a glance (presenting information clearly). Every one of these steps is statistics at work.

### Recording Data with Tally Marks and Frequency Tables

Statistics is built on data — the facts and numbers you gather when you count or record something. Before you can study data you have to collect it, and there are four common ways to do that:

1. **Observation** — you watch and note down what you see.
2. **Counting** — you count the items one by one.
3. **Interview** — you ask someone a question and record their answer.
4. **Questionnaire** — you give someone a written list of questions to answer.

Once you have collected your data, you need a neat way to record it as it comes in. That is where **tally marks** are used. A tally mark is a single stroke that stands for one item. You add one stroke for each item you collect, and every fifth stroke is drawn across the previous four. So five items are written as a bundle of four strokes with a fifth crossing them. Grouping the marks in fives makes counting fast, because you can count the bundles of five and then the leftovers.

Suppose you stand by the road and note the vehicles that pass, in this order:

bus, car, car, car, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, car, car, lorry, bicycle, car

You record each vehicle with a tally mark under its name as it passes. When you finish, your tally looks like this:

| Vehicles | Tally | Total |
|---|---|---|
| car | \|\|\|\|/ \| | 6 |
| bus | \| | 1 |
| lorry | \|\| | 2 |
| bicycle | \|\|\| | 3 |
| motor bike | | 0 |

Let's check the tally. **car** appears six times in the data, so it earns one full group of five (four strokes with the fifth across them) and one more mark on its own — that is 6, and the total column shows 6. **bus** appears once, **lorry** twice, and **bicycle** three times, so each gets the matching number of marks. **motor bike** never appears, so it gets no marks and its total is 0. Add the totals and you get 6 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 0 = 12, which matches the 12 vehicles listed.

When your tally is complete, you can present it in a **frequency table**. A frequency table is a table showing how many times each item occurs in a set of data. It brings all the totals together into one clear summary.

Here is a frequency table showing the favourite colours of a group of students:

| colour | frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

The **frequency** is simply how many students chose that colour. Reading this table, you can immediately see that Pink is the most popular colour (30 students chose it) and Green is the least popular (only 14 chose it). A frequency table lets you read the whole set of data at a glance, so you can compare items and answer questions about the data easily.
