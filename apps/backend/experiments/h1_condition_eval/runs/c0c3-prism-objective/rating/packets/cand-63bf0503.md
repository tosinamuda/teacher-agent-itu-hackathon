## The revision pack

### Why We Need Statistics

Think about how much information surrounds you every day. In one morning you might see how many cars pass your house, how many goals your favourite team scored last season, or how much your school spends on books each term. All of these are numbers, but numbers on their own are not very useful. The job of statistics is to turn those scattered numbers into something you can understand and use.

<b>Statistics</b> is the branch of mathematics that deals with collecting, organising, analysing, and interpreting data. <b>Data</b> simply means the information or facts that you collect, usually as numbers. The word statistics can mean two things: the whole process of handling data, or the numbers themselves once they have been organised.

<b>What is the purpose of statistics?</b> The main purpose is to make sense of information so that we can make better decisions. Statistics helps us in several ways:

<ul>
<li><b>To summarise large amounts of information.</b> If your teacher lists the marks of all 40 students in your class, that is a lot of numbers. Statistics lets you group them into a table so you can quickly see, for example, how many students scored above 60.</li>
<li><b>To compare.</b> Statistics lets you compare things fairly, such as the attendance of two classes or the rainfall of two months.</li>
<li><b>To spot patterns and trends.</b> When data is organised, you can see whether something is increasing, decreasing, or staying the same over time.</li>
<li><b>To support decisions.</b> Governments, companies, and schools all use statistics to plan. A shop uses sales data to decide which goods to stock; a school uses exam data to decide where learners need more help.</li>
<li><b>To predict.</b> From organised data, you can often make a reasonable guess about what might happen next.</li>
</ul>

<b>Collecting and tabulating data.</b> The first step in statistics is gathering the data. You can collect it by counting, by measuring, or by asking questions. Once you have the raw data — that is, the data just as you collected it, in no particular order — you organise it into a <b>table</b>. Tabulating means arranging the data into rows and columns so that it is neat and easy to read. A well-made table is the starting point for all the analysis that comes later.</p>

<b>Worked example 1: Seeing the purpose of statistics.</b>

Suppose your school wants to buy sports kits and needs to know which sport learners prefer. The school asks 20 learners and records each learner's choice:

Football, Tennis, Football, Swimming, Tennis, Football, Swimming, Football, Tennis, Football,
Swimming, Football, Tennis, Football, Football, Swimming, Tennis, Football, Swimming, Football

These are the <i>raw data</i> — 20 answers in the order they were collected. On their own they are hard to read. Now let's tabulate them. We count how many learners chose each sport.

Football appears 10 times, Tennis appears 4 times, and Swimming appears 6 times.

<table border="1" cellpadding="5">
<tr><th>Sport</th><th>Number of learners (frequency)</th></tr>
<tr><td>Football</td><td>10</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tennis</td><td>4</td></tr>
<tr><td>Swimming</td><td>6</td></tr>
<tr><td><b>Total</b></td><td><b>20</b></td></tr>
</table>

The table immediately shows the purpose of statistics: the messy list has been summarised into a clear picture. We can see at a glance that Football is the most popular and Tennis is the least popular. With this organised data, the school can decide how many kits of each sport to buy. Notice also that the frequencies add up to 20, which confirms we counted every learner.</p>

<b>Worked example 2: Reading and interpreting a table.</b>

A farmer records the number of eggs collected from her hens on each day of one week.

<table border="1" cellpadding="5">
<tr><th>Day</th><th>Number of eggs</th></tr>
<tr><td>Monday</td><td>25</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tuesday</td><td>28</td></tr>
<tr><td>Wednesday</td><td>22</td></tr>
<tr><td>Thursday</td><td>30</td></tr>
<tr><td>Friday</td><td>26</td></tr>
<tr><td>Saturday</td><td>24</td></tr>
<tr><td>Sunday</td><td>27</td></tr>
</table>

What can this table tell us? Interpreting means reading meaning out of the organised data.

<ul>
<li>Thursday had the highest number of eggs, with 30.</li>
<li>Wednesday had the lowest number of eggs, with 22.</li>
<li>The eggs collected each day are between 22 and 30, so the hens are fairly steady producers.</li>
</ul>

Now suppose the farmer wants to know roughly how many eggs she gets in a week. She can add the numbers: 25 + 28 + 22 + 30 + 26 + 24 + 27 = 182. So in one week the hens produced 182 eggs. This total is itself a useful statistic. The farmer could compare this week's total with next week's to spot whether the hens are producing more or fewer eggs — a trend that helps her plan for selling the eggs.</p>

### Reading Tables to Make Sense of Data

After data is collected, it is often arranged into a table. A table is a tidy way of showing information in rows and columns so that the numbers are easy to read and compare. Reading a table means looking carefully at its headings to understand what each row and each column stands for, and then pulling out the information you need to answer a question.

Let's look at the parts of a table. The top row (the heading row) tells you what each column contains. The first column usually names the things being counted, and the other columns hold the numbers. To interpret a table, ask yourself three things:

1. What does the table count? (Look at the headings.)
2. Which row or column do I need? (Find the right place.)
3. What does the number actually mean? (Read it with the heading.)

Below is a table showing how 40 students in a school travel to school each morning.

The table below shows how 40 students travel to school.

| Mode of travel | Number of students |
|----------------|--------------------|
| Walking        | 18                 |
| Bicycle        | 10                 |
| Bus            | 8                  |
| Car            | 4                  |

We can use this table to answer questions.

Example 1: How many students walk to school?
Find the row "Walking". The number beside it is 18. So 18 students walk.

Example 2: How many more students walk than come by car?
Walking is 18 and car is 4. Difference: 18 − 4 = 14. So 14 more students walk than come by car.

Example 3: The total of all the numbers is 18 + 10 + 8 + 4 = 40, which matches the total number of students. This is a quick way to check that the table is correct.

So a table lets us find exact numbers and compare them quickly.
