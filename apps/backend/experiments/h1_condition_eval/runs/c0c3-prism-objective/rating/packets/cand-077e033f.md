### Plane Shapes and Their Properties

A plane shape is a flat, two-dimensional (2-D) shape. It is called two-dimensional because it has only length and width — it has no thickness. Plane shapes include triangles, squares, rectangles, parallelograms, trapeziums, circles and many other polygons. In this section you will learn to name these shapes, state their properties, and notice their similarities and differences.

A triangle is a closed shape with three straight sides and three angles. Triangles come in three types, and you can tell which is which by looking at their sides and angles (see fig-01).

An <strong>isosceles</strong> triangle has two adjacent sides equal, the two base angles equal, and one line of symmetry.

A <strong>scalene</strong> triangle has no two sides equal, no two angles equal, and no line of symmetry.

An <strong>equilateral</strong> triangle has all three sides equal, all three angles equal (each is 60°), and three lines of symmetry.

So the three types are similar because they all have three sides and three angles, but they differ in how many sides and angles are equal.

*[figure: Isosceles: a single tick on each of the two slanted sides, an arc at each base angle. Scalene: one, two and three ticks on the three sides, an arc at each vertex. Equilateral: a single tick on each of the three sides, an arc at each vertex.]*

A square and a rectangle are both four-sided shapes whose angles are all equal (each is 90°) and that both have two pairs of parallel sides (see fig-02). Their difference is in the sides.

A <strong>square</strong> has all four sides equal, four lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other at a right angle.

A <strong>rectangle</strong> has two opposite sides equal (two longer sides and two shorter sides), two lines of symmetry, and its diagonals bisect each other but not at a right angle.

The two shapes are similar because both have all right angles and two pairs of parallel sides. They differ because the square's four sides are all equal, while the rectangle's opposite sides are equal.

*[figure: Square: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the four sides. Rectangle: a right-angle mark at each corner, a single tick on each of the two longer sides and a double tick on each of the two shorter sides.]*

A parallelogram and a trapezium are both four-sided shapes, but they differ in how many sides are parallel (see fig-03).

A <strong>parallelogram</strong> has two pairs of opposite sides equal and parallel, its opposite angles are equal, it has no line of symmetry, and its diagonals divide it into two equal triangles.

A <strong>trapezium</strong> has only one pair of parallel sides, its opposite angles are not equal, it has no line of symmetry, and a diagonal divides it into two unequal triangles.

So both shapes have no line of symmetry, but the parallelogram has two pairs of parallel sides while the trapezium has only one.

*[figure: Parallelogram: a single arrow on each of the two horizontal sides, a double arrow on each of the two slanted sides. Trapezium: a single arrow on each of the two parallel sides, and one diagonal drawn in.]*

A polygon is a closed shape that has three or more straight sides. Polygons are named by their number of sides. You can also work out how many triangles a polygon can be divided into: it is always two fewer than the number of sides, that is (number of sides) − 2.

| Name of polygon | Number of sides | Number of triangles (n − 2) |
|---|---|---|
| Triangle | 3 | 1 |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 2 |
| Pentagon | 5 | 3 |
| Hexagon | 6 | 4 |
| Heptagon | 7 | 5 |
| Octagon | 8 | 6 |
| Nonagon | 9 | 7 |
| Decagon | 10 | 8 |
| Hendecagon (undecagon) | 11 | 9 |
| Dodecagon | 12 | 10 |

Use the table to name any polygon from its number of sides, and use the rule (sides − 2) to find how many triangles it can be divided into.

<strong>Example 1.</strong> Identify each shape below and state one property of each.

<em>(a)</em> A shape with all three sides equal.
<em>Answer:</em> This is an <strong>equilateral triangle</strong>. Its three angles are all equal (60° each) and it has three lines of symmetry.

<em>(b)</em> A four-sided shape with two pairs of parallel sides and all angles 90°, whose four sides are not all equal.
<em>Answer:</em> This is a <strong>rectangle</strong>. It has two lines of symmetry and its diagonals bisect each other but not at a right angle.

<strong>Example 2.</strong> How many triangles can a dodecagon (12 sides) be divided into?

<em>Answer:</em> Use the rule: number of triangles = number of sides − 2 = 12 − 2 = 10.

So a dodecagon can be divided into 10 triangles.

### Parts of a Circle

A circle is the set of all points that are the same distance from a fixed point at its middle. That fixed point is the <strong>centre</strong> of the circle. Look at the two circles in fig-04 as you read the names below — every part is labelled on them.

The <strong>circumference</strong> is the whole curved boundary of the circle. Think of it as the perimeter of the circle — the distance all the way around the edge.

A <strong>radius</strong> is any straight line that joins the centre of the circle to the circumference. In fig-04 a radius is drawn from the centre down to the edge. Because every point on the circumference is the same distance from the centre, all the radii of one circle are equal in length.

A <strong>diameter</strong> is any straight line that passes right across the circle, through the centre, from one point on the circumference to another. It is the longest straight line you can draw across a circle, and it is twice as long as a radius.

A <strong>chord</strong> is any straight line joining two points on the circumference. A chord does not have to pass through the centre — only a chord that passes through the centre is called a diameter. So every diameter is a chord, but a chord is a diameter only when it crosses the centre.

An <strong>arc</strong> is any curved part of the circumference. A small piece of the circumference is an arc, just as a section of a straight line is part of that line.

The remaining names pick out parts of the circle's inside and outside:
- A <strong>segment</strong> is the region bounded by a chord and the arc it cuts off — in fig-04 the part above the chord is shaded.
- A <strong>sector</strong> is the region bounded by two radii and the arc between them, like a slice of the circle. In fig-04 the shaded slice below is a sector.
- A <strong>tangent</strong> is a straight line that touches the circle at exactly one point and does not cut across it. In fig-04 it is the line touching the right-hand side of the second circle.

*[figure: Two labelled circles. The first has the diameter drawn across it and a radius from its centre to the circumference, with the arc and the circumference labelled. The second has a chord near the top with the segment above it shaded, a shaded sector below with its right-hand bounding line labelled Radius, and a tangent touching it on the right.]*

A circle has a radius of 4 cm. Answer these about the same circle.

<em>a) What is the length of its diameter?</em><br>
A diameter passes through the centre and reaches the circumference on both sides, so it is twice the radius. 4 cm × 2 = <strong>8 cm</strong>.

<em>b) Is the diameter a chord? Is it an arc?</em><br>
The diameter is a straight line joining two points on the circumference, so yes, it is a chord. It is not curved, so it is not an arc — an arc is a curved part of the circumference.

<em>c) A straight line touches this circle at exactly one point. What is it called?</em><br>
A line that touches a circle at exactly one point without crossing it is a <strong>tangent</strong>.

### Finding the Perimeter of Plane Shapes

The **perimeter** of a shape is the total distance around it — you simply walk round the outside and add up every side you cross. In this section you will find the perimeter of a regular polygon, a square, a rectangle, a trapezium, a parallelogram and a circle, and you will also learn to work backwards to find a missing side or radius when the perimeter is already known.

A **square** has four equal sides. If each side is $L$, then its perimeter is

$$L + L + L + L = 4L.$$

A **rectangle** has two long sides (length $L$) and two short sides (breadth $b$), so its perimeter is

$$L + b + L + b = 2L + 2b = 2(L + b).$$

A **regular** polygon has all sides equal *and* all angles equal. (Equal sides alone are not enough — a rhombus has four equal sides but its angles are not all equal, so it is not regular.) If a regular polygon has $n$ sides, each of length $s$, its perimeter is $n \times s$.

**Worked example — square.** A square has sides of 15 cm. Its perimeter is

$$L + L + L + L = 4L = 4 \times 15 = 60\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Worked example — rectangle.** A rectangle has length 10 cm and breadth 7 cm. Its perimeter is

$$2L + 2b = 2(10) + 2(7) = 2(17) = 34\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Worked example — finding a missing breadth.** The perimeter of a rectangle is 60 cm and its length is 17 cm. What is its breadth?

Use $P = 2L + 2b$ with $P = 60$ and $L = 17$:

$$60 = 2(17) + 2b = 34 + 2b.$$

Collect like terms:

$$60 - 34 = 2b \quad\Rightarrow\quad 26 = 2b.$$

Divide both sides by 2:

$$b = \frac{26}{2} = 13\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

So the breadth is **13 cm**.

**Worked example — trapezium.** A trapezium has four sides. Just add all four lengths. For a trapezium with sides 11 cm, 9 cm, 5 cm and 4 cm:

$$11 + 9 + 5 + 4 = 29\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

(There is no special formula here — different trapezia have different side lengths, so the perimeter is always found by adding the four sides you are given.)

**Worked example — parallelogram.** A parallelogram has two pairs of equal sides. If the base $a$ is 9 cm and the slanted side $b$ is 5 cm:

$$a + a + b + b = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b) = 2(9 + 5) = 2(14) = 28\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Worked example — regular hexagon.** A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides. If each side is 5.2 cm:

$$\text{perimeter} = 6 \times 5.2 = 31.2\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

The perimeter of a circle is called its **circumference**. It is given by

$$\text{circumference} = 2\pi r \quad \text{or} \quad \pi d,$$

where $r$ is the radius and $d$ is the diameter (twice the radius). You are usually told which value of $\pi$ to use, such as $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$ or $\pi = 3.14$.

**Worked example — circumference.** A circle has radius 7 cm. Using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$:

$$2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = 2 \times 22 = 44\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

You could get the same answer through the diameter: $d = 2 \times 7 = 14\ \mathrm{cm}$, so

$$\pi d = \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 22 \times 2 = 44\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Worked example — finding the radius from the circumference.** The circumference of a circle is 440 cm. Find its radius, using $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$.

$$\text{circumference} = 2\pi r \quad\Rightarrow\quad 440 = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times r = \frac{44r}{7}.$$

Cross-multiply:

$$440 \times 7 = 44r \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3080 = 44r.$$

Divide both sides by 44:

$$r = \frac{3080}{44} = 70\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Worked example — a partial shape.** A shape is a quarter circle: two straight sides of 4.9 cm meeting at a right angle, closed by a curved arc. Its perimeter is the two straight sides plus the curved part.

The curved part is one quarter of a full circle:

$$\frac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r = \frac{1}{4} \times 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 4.9 = 7.7\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

So the perimeter is

$$4.9 + 4.9 + 7.7 = 17.5\ \mathrm{cm}.$$

**Finding the Perimeter of Plane Shapes**

1. 32 cm

   Perimeter of a square $= 4L = 4 \times 8 = 32\ \mathrm{cm}$.

2. 22 cm

   $2L + 2b = 2(7) + 2(4) = 14 + 8 = 22\ \mathrm{cm}$, or $2(7 + 4) = 2(11) = 22\ \mathrm{cm}$.

3. 25 cm

   A regular pentagon has 5 equal sides, so perimeter $= 5 \times 5 = 25\ \mathrm{cm}$.

4. 30 cm

   A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides, so perimeter $= 6 \times 5 = 30\ \mathrm{cm}$.

5. 22 cm

   $2(a + b) = 2(6 + 5) = 2(11) = 22\ \mathrm{cm}$.

6. $6d$

   Length $= 2d$, breadth $= d$. Perimeter $= 2(\text{length} + \text{breadth}) = 2(2d + d) = 2(3d) = 6d$.
