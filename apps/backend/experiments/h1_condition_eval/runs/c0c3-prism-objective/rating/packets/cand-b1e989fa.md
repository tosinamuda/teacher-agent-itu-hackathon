## The revision pack

### Cylinders, Spheres, and Finding Volume

Two solid shapes you meet all the time are the cylinder and the sphere. Let's look closely at what makes each one special, so you can pick them out anywhere.

A <b>cylinder</b> has two identical flat circular faces, one at the top and one at the bottom. These two circles are called the <b>bases</b>. The two bases are joined by a single curved surface that wraps all the way around. A cylinder has no sharp points (no vertices), but it does have two edges — the two circular rims where the curved surface meets each base. Because of that curved surface, a cylinder can roll along the ground, but only in a straight line (it rolls end over end, not in all directions). Think of a tin of baked beans or a drinking glass.

A <b>sphere</b> is perfectly round — every point on its surface is exactly the same distance from its centre. That fixed distance is called the <b>radius</b>. A sphere has no flat faces, no edges, and no vertices at all. Because it has only one continuous curved surface, a sphere rolls in every direction. Think of a football or a tennis ball.

Here is a quick way to tell them apart: a sphere is the only shape that rolls freely in every direction and has no flat face, no edge, and no corner. A cylinder has exactly two flat circular faces, two circular edges, and no corners.

Volume is the amount of space a solid takes up, and we measure it in cubic units such as cm³ or m³. Three box-like solids have a simple volume formula you can rely on.

A <b>cube</b> is a solid where every edge is the same length. If its edge is <i>s</i>, then its volume is:
<b>Volume of cube = s × s × s = s³</b>

A <b>cuboid</b> (a rectangular box) has three different edges — length <i>l</i>, width <i>w</i> and height <i>h</i>. Its volume is:
<b>Volume of cuboid = l × w × h</b>

A <b>prism</b> is any solid that has the same shape all the way through from one end to the other. The two ends are identical polygons, and they are joined by flat rectangular faces. The volume of any prism is:
<b>Volume of prism = area of the cross‑section × length</b>

The cross‑section is the shape of the two identical ends — for example, if a prism has a triangular end, you first find the area of that triangle and then multiply by the prism's length. So a cuboid is actually a prism whose cross‑section is a rectangle, and the rule "area of cross‑section × length" gives the same result as l × w × h.

<b>Worked Example 1 — Properties of a cylinder</b>
A tin of soup has two flat circular ends and one curved surface that joins them.
How many faces, edges and vertices does it have?
The two circular ends are flat faces, and the curved surface is not flat but it is still one surface. So there are <b>3 faces</b> in total. There are two circular edges — the rim where the curved surface meets each circular base. There are <b>no vertices</b>, because no flat faces meet at a point.

<b>Worked Example 2 — Volume of a cube</b>
Find the volume of a cube with edge 4 cm.
Volume = s × s × s = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³.

<b>Worked Example 3 — Volume of a cuboid</b>
A box is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide and 2 cm high. Find its volume.
Volume = l × w × h = 5 × 3 × 2 = 30 cm³.

<b>Worked Example 4 — Volume of a prism</b>
A triangular prism has a triangular end whose area is 8 cm² and a length of 6 cm. Find its volume.
Volume = area of cross‑section × length = 8 × 6 = 48 cm³.

### Volume of Cubes, Cuboids and Prisms

Volume is the amount of space a solid takes up. It is measured in cubic units, like cubic centimetres (cm³) or cubic metres (m³).

A **cube** has every edge the same length. If the edge is \(a\), then:
\[ \text{Volume} = a \times a \times a = a^3 \]

A **cuboid** (a rectangular box) has three different edge lengths: length \(l\), width \(w\) and height \(h\). Its volume is:
\[ \text{Volume} = l \times w \times h \]

A **prism** is a solid with the same shape (a face) all the way through. A cube and a cuboid are both prisms. For any prism, the volume is found by multiplying the area of the end face (the cross-section) by the length (or height):
\[ \text{Volume} = \text{area of cross-section} \times \text{length} \]

So the same rule works for a cube, a cuboid and any other prism: find the area of the end face, then multiply by how long the prism is.

**Example 1: a cube**
A cube has edges of length 5 cm. Find its volume.

\[ \text{Volume} = a^3 = 5 \times 5 \times 5 = 125 \]
The volume is **125 cm³**.

**Example 2: a cuboid**
A cuboid is 8 cm long, 3 cm wide and 4 cm high. Find its volume.

\[ \text{Volume} = l \times w \times h = 8 \times 3 \times 4 = 96 \]
The volume is **96 cm³**.

**Example 3: a prism**
A triangular prism has a triangular end face with area 12 cm² and is 7 cm long. Find its volume.

\[ \text{Volume} = \text{area of cross-section} \times \text{length} = 12 \times 7 = 84 \]
The volume is **84 cm³**.

**Practice**

1. A cube has edges of length 6 cm. What is its volume?

2. A cuboid is 10 cm long, 4 cm wide and 3 cm high. What is its volume?

3. A prism has an end face with area 15 cm² and is 8 cm long. What is its volume?
