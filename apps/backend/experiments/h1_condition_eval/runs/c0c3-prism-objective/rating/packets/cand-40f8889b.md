## The revision pack

### What Statistics Is For

Whenever you need to know something about a group — how many pupils are in your class, how many bottle tops you have, which scores your classmates got in a test — you are working with <strong>data</strong>. Data are facts or information collected for a particular purpose. The collection on its own is not enough: the information is only useful once you can see what it means.

<strong>Statistics</strong> is the science of collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing and interpreting data. It is the whole journey your information takes, from the moment you gather it to the moment you understand what it tells you.

Statistics matters because it helps you to:

- <strong>Collect information</strong> — gather the facts you need.
- <strong>Organize information</strong> — arrange it so it is easy to look through.
- <strong>Compare information</strong> — see how one thing stands against another.
- <strong>Make decisions</strong> — choose sensibly from what the information shows.
- <strong>Present information clearly</strong> — show it so others can understand it too.

A single fact is just a fact. But when you collect facts, organize them and present them clearly, they become something you can compare and decide on. That is the purpose of statistics.

### From Raw Data to a Frequency Table

Very often we want to answer a question about a group of things, like "which vehicle passes our school most often?" or "what is the favourite colour in our class?". To answer that, we first need data — that is, pieces of information we have collected. Data can be collected in a few different ways:

Four common ways to collect data are:
<ul>
<li><strong>Observation</strong> — watching and noting what you see, for example watching the road to record passing vehicles.</li>
<li><strong>Counting</strong> — counting the items you want to know about, for example counting the pencils on each desk.</li>
<li><strong>Interview</strong> — asking people questions and recording their answers.</li>
<li><strong>Questionnaire</strong> — giving people a written list of questions to answer.</li>
</ul>

Once you have collected data, you need a neat way to record it as it comes in. That is what <strong>tally marks</strong> are for. A tally is a small stroke | written for each item you record. When you reach the fifth stroke, you draw it across the other four, like this: <strong>||||/</strong> (which counts as 5).

When the tallying is finished, we can count the strokes for each item and write down how many times it occurred. A <strong>frequency table</strong> is a table that shows how many times each item occurs in a set of data. The number of times an item occurs is called its <strong>frequency</strong>. For example, the favourite colours of a group of students can be shown in a frequency table:

| Colour | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Red | 18 |
| Blue | 20 |
| Green | 14 |
| Yellow | 16 |
| White | 15 |
| Pink | 30 |

Let's put this all together with the data collected by watching vehicles pass:
<p>car, car, car, car, car, car, bus, lorry, lorry, bicycle, bicycle, bicycle</p>
We record each vehicle with a tally mark as it appears. The tally for "car" builds up as six strokes, which we group as five and one extra: <strong>||||/ |</strong>, so its total is 6. The other vehicles follow the same way.

Now we read the totals from the tally to make a frequency table. The vehicle that occurs most often has the largest frequency, and the one that occurs least often has the smallest:

| Vehicles | Tally | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| car | \|\|\|\|\|/ \| | 6 |
| bus | \| | 1 |
| lorry | \|\| | 2 |
| bicycle | \|\|\| | 3 |

Reading the table tells us: car appeared 6 times, bus 1 time, lorry 2 times and bicycle 3 times. The car is the most common vehicle, and the bus is the least common.
