Write the plan in parts. First the lesson's own part: the introduction, the evaluation and the
lesson-wide lists. Then one part for each of the lesson's curriculum objectives: the lesson
objectives that teach it, each with its core step and its check questions. The steps run in
this order: the introduction, the core steps in the order the parts give them, the evaluation.

Write no IDs and no links. A core step belongs to the objective it sits under, and a check
question checks that objective. Where a part sits is the link.

## Objectives

- Teach the lesson's curriculum objectives and nothing outside them.
- Write at most 4 lesson objectives in the whole lesson. With more curriculum objectives than
  that, teach the closely related ones together, under one of them.
- Give a curriculum objective an empty list of lesson objectives only when the lesson does not
  teach it.
- State each lesson objective as what the students will be able to do by the end of the
  lesson. Where the teacher's source states an objective, keep its words.
- Give each objective its kind of knowledge:
  - **concept**: a definition or idea, such as "what a prime number is".
  - **procedure**: a step-by-step method, such as "add fractions with a common denominator".
  - **representation**: reading or drawing a form, such as a diagram, number line or table.
    Give the form the students start from and the form they change it to, such as
    source_form "fraction", target_form "point on a number line".
- Give each objective its focus: the exact skill or idea it teaches. Two objectives under one
  curriculum objective differ in focus, or in their forms. "Add fractions" and "Add fractions"
  is wrong; "Add fractions with a common denominator" and "Add fractions with different
  denominators" is right.
- Teach at least one thing that is new. What the class already knows is recalled in the
  introduction, not taught again as an objective.

## Steps

- Each lesson objective has exactly one core step, and it teaches that objective only.
- The introduction teaches no lesson objective. It recalls what the lesson builds on and frames
  what the lesson will teach, in a few minutes.
- Give every step a title, a short summary for the teacher, its minutes and its activities.
- Every step takes 1 minute or more. All the steps together fit in the lesson's time: a 40
  minute lesson cannot hold steps of 10, 20, 15 and 10 minutes.
- Before you write a core step, its blocks or its activities, read
  `references/steps-and-activities.md`.

## Evaluation

- Every lesson objective has at least one check question. The evaluation is made of these
  questions and nothing else.
- Before you write a check question, an assessment or the evaluation step, read
  `references/evaluation-questions.md`.

## The lesson-wide lists

- Before you write the entry behaviour, instructional materials, misconceptions, references or
  theme, read `references/lesson-context.md`.

## The classroom

- Keep numbers and language right for the class's grade.
- Use Nigerian contexts, and naira where money is involved.
- Use the names the class is taught, such as BODMAS for the order of operations.

## The lesson's sources

Read the lesson's resource before you write. Its `curriculum` holds the curriculum's own notes
for the lesson. Its `textbook` holds every textbook record the lesson has, with the text of each
(`records`), and, for each curriculum objective, the records it links to and the role each plays
in teaching it (`by_objective`). A record that no objective links is shared foundation for the
whole lesson.

- Where the teacher gave a document, write from it first and keep its words, its order and its
  method. Use the curriculum and the textbook only for what the document leaves out.
- Where there is no document, write from the curriculum and the textbook. Base each core step on
  the records its objective links to: an `instruction` or `worked_example` record is the
  teaching to follow, a `foundation` record is what the students already know, and an
  `exercise` record is where the practice questions come from.
- Take the activities and the instructional materials from the curriculum's teacher and student
  activities and its materials, and make them concrete for this lesson. Take the entry
  behaviour from the curriculum's entry behaviour.
- The textbook's own check question for an objective is a good model for a check question; write
  your own in the same kind, and do not copy it word for word.
- Name every textbook record you used in the references, by its record id and its title, for
  example "ch14-b021: Bisecting a line segment". A reference that names no record of the lesson
  is refused. Never name a source the lesson's resource does not hold.

## Revising a plan

A teacher may point at part of the plan. A step is named by its step id: `intro`, `evaluation`,
or `core1`, `core2` and so on, the core step of the first lesson objective, the second, and so
on in the order the parts give them. A block is named `<step id>-b<n>`, the nth block of that
step. A lesson objective is named `lo1`, `lo2` and so on in the same order. Find the part the
teacher pointed at, change it, and leave the parts they did not point at as they are.

`revise_part` replaces a part whole. Read the draft, and send the part as it stands with your
change made, every field of it. The lesson's own part is the unit `lesson`: its introduction and
evaluation are inside it, and it holds no lesson objectives.

Where the teacher only asks a question, answer it and change nothing.
